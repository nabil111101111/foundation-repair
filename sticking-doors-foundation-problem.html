Sticking Doors: When It's the Foundation and When It's the Weather

A sticking door is a foundation symptom only some of the time. Doors that stick in the same weeks every year and free up again usually point to humidity swelling the wood, or to seasonal clay movement that reverses itself. Doors that stick a little worse every season, sit visibly out of square, or drag hard on one corner point to progressive settlement, and that is the pattern that deserves a professional evaluation.

Doors and windows report ground movement earlier than almost anything else in a house, because they were fitted square with very small gaps. The ground only has to move a fraction of an inch to close those gaps unevenly. That makes a sticking door a useful early signal, but a weak verdict on its own: the job of this page is to show you how to read the pattern before you spend money on either carpentry or piers.

You will find the seasonal-versus-progressive table first, then the soil causes by region, a ten-minute audit you can repeat each season, and what the fix costs when settlement really is the cause. FoundationMatch is a matching service, not a contractor, so the emphasis stays on diagnosis and published price ranges you can check any quote against.

Interior and exterior doors carry different evidence. An exterior door sits in the wall that stands on the foundation, so its frame reports foundation movement fairly directly. An interior door on an upper floor is two layers of framing away from the footing, and its sticking is much more often humidity, hinge wear, or a house settling as a whole. Weigh the exterior doors first.

Seasonal sticking or progressive sticking? The pattern decides

Start with timing. Seasonal sticking follows the calendar and the weather: the same doors misbehave in the same months, then recover. Progressive sticking follows a trend instead: it starts mild, recovers less each cycle, and eventually the door needs force in every month of the year. One pattern is the house breathing. The other is the house moving.

Location is the second clue. Humidity swells door slabs fairly evenly and can strike anywhere in the house, including interior doors on upper floors. Settlement clusters: the sticking doors gather on one side or in one corner, the same side where floors slope and cracks appear. Draw the pattern on a floor plan and it often maps the low corner of the foundation for you. Keep that sketch; it becomes the baseline every later check is compared against, and it is the single most useful page you can hand an evaluator.

Windows add a useful cross-check because their frames rack in the same walls but their materials swell differently. A vinyl or aluminium window that binds is rarely swollen by humidity, so a window that starts sticking in step with a nearby door strengthens the movement theory. A wooden sash window sticking alone in August, with doors behaving, is usually just a wooden sash window in August.

Can foundation problems cause doors to stick?

Yes. When one part of a foundation settles or heaves, the wall framing above it racks out of square, and a door that swung freely starts to rub the frame, drag at one corner, or miss its latch. Foundation-related sticking usually affects several doors on the same side of the house and comes with diagonal cracks above the frames.

How do I tell if door sticking is foundation movement or just humidity?

Check the calendar and the gaps. Humidity sticking peaks in the most humid weeks, affects wooden doors more than their frames, and reverses in dry weather. Foundation sticking follows soil conditions instead, worsens in one direction over time, and shows uneven gaps: wide at the top on one side, pinched at the bottom. A dated log separates the two within a season.

Why doors and windows show movement first

Doors and windows are the most sensitive instruments in your house, and they are already installed. A door slab hangs in a frame fitted square, with an even reveal of about 1/8 inch along the top and sides. The frame is fixed to framing that stands on the foundation. When the foundation moves even slightly, the frame racks into a faint parallelogram, the reveal closes at one corner, and the door is the first thing in the house to announce it.

Windows report the same movement in their own way. A sash that used to slide begins to bind, a casement crank grows stiff, or a fine diagonal crack opens in the drywall above a window corner. Drywall is brittle and tears along the same stress lines, so a sticking window plus a fresh diagonal crack above it is a stronger signal than either symptom alone. Neither sign needs a specialist to spot; both need a date written beside them to become evidence.

The latch tells the same story in miniature. A door closes through its frame in a fixed arc, and the strike plate on the frame side has only a small pocket of tolerance. When the frame racks, the latch bolt starts landing high or low against the plate, and you feel it as a door that needs a lift or a hip-check to close. Note which direction you have to push; it matches the direction the frame has moved.

Why do my doors stick in summer but work fine in winter?

Two seasonal effects stack up. Wooden doors and frames absorb summer humidity and swell slightly, and in clay country the soil itself swells when it is wet, lifting that side of the house a fraction. Both reverse as conditions dry out. Seasonal sticking that fully reverses is common and usually needs no structural work; log it and watch whether each summer gets worse.

What does it mean when the gap above a door is uneven?

An uneven gap means the frame has racked. If the gap is wide at the top on one side and pinched at the top corner on the other, that side of the house has likely dropped, or the opposite side has lifted. Measure both top corners and write the numbers down with a date; a growing difference over months points to active movement.

The soil causes underneath sticking doors

In Shreveport and Abilene, expansive clay is the usual suspect. The soil swells when wet and shrinks in drought, and because weather rarely wets or dries a whole lot evenly, one side moves more than the other. Homeowners describe doors that go out of square every spring, then drag again by late summer. That rhythm, repeated for years, also fatigues mortar and drywall, which is why clay movement arrives as a cluster of small symptoms rather than one big one.

Other regions move differently. New Orleans sits on subsiding delta soil, so sticking there tends to worsen steadily rather than seasonally. Around Lexington, a dissolved limestone void can drop one corner with little warning. In Iowa and Wisconsin, frost can lift a shallow footing in a hard winter and set it back down slightly off in spring. A slow plumbing leak can imitate every one of these by washing soil out from under a single stretch of footing.

Water management quietly drives many seasonal cases. Where grading, gutters, or a downspout keep one side of a house wetter than the other, the soil on that side stays swollen while the dry side shrinks, and the house rocks gently between the two states. Doors on the boundary between the wet side and the dry side feel it first. Correcting drainage sometimes calms seasonal sticking before any structural work is discussed.

One cause that fools people is the house's own success at staying dry on one side. A driveway, patio, or covered porch can shelter the soil beside a footing from rain for decades while the exposed side cycles wet and dry with every season. The sheltered side stays shrunk, the open side swells, and the doors above the boundary rack a little more each year even though nothing dramatic ever happened. Evaluators look for that asymmetry, and it is one reason a walk around the outside of the house is part of every serious inspection.

My doors started sticking after a long drought. Is that a foundation problem?

It can be, especially on clay soil. Drought pulls moisture from the ground, the clay shrinks, and the footing above it drops until rain returns the moisture. If the doors recover fully after wet weather, you likely watched the soil breathe. If they recover less each cycle, or the soil has pulled away from the foundation in a visible gap, get an evaluation.

Why do the doors on one side of my house stick while the other side is fine?

Because settlement is rarely even. When one side or corner drops and the rest stays put, the framing tilts, and every door on the dropping side racks the same way. That one-sided pattern is useful evidence: sketch your sticking doors on a rough floor plan and you have drawn a map of where the movement is, which is exactly what any evaluator will want to see.

Check it yourself: the ten-minute door audit

You do not need instruments for this, just consistency. Give each sticking door the same few checks, then repeat them at the start of each season. What you are building is a trend, and a trend is what separates a free humidity problem from a settlement problem while there is still time to choose repairs calmly. Ten minutes per season is the whole cost.

Repeatability matters more than precision. Measure the top gap at both corners with the same ruler, stand in the same place for the photograph, and write the weather beside the date, because a gap measured at the end of a drought and a gap measured after a wet month are supposed to differ. The audit only means something when the method stays the same.

Bring the audit with you when you call. A dated log with gap measurements, weather notes, and a floor-plan sketch lets an evaluator separate a seasonal nuisance from active settlement in one visit, and it protects you from the opposite error as well: paying for piers to cure a humidity problem. Homeowners who arrive with records consistently get straighter answers than homeowners who arrive with a feeling.

  • List every sticking door and window on a simple floor-plan sketch
  • Note the season and the recent weather when sticking starts and stops
  • Measure the top gap at both corners of each problem door and record the numbers
  • Check for diagonal cracks above the frames of the worst doors
  • Photograph one door gap with a coin in frame for scale, and repeat from the same spot

How long should I monitor sticking doors before calling someone?

One full seasonal cycle is the useful minimum, because seasonal sticking proves or clears itself by reversing. Skip the wait and call sooner if a door suddenly will not open, gaps change noticeably between checks, new cracks appear above frames, or several doors on one side worsen together. Those are progressive signs, and progressive movement is cheaper to stop early.

What it costs to fix sticking doors that come from settlement

If your log says humidity, the fix is carpentry, not concrete: longer hinge screws, a planed edge, a strike plate shifted a fraction. If the log says settlement, treating the door treats the symptom while the house keeps moving, and the real repair sits underneath it. Piers that stabilise the dropped side run $1,000 to $2,500 each in published ranges, and a typical structural repair totals $4,000 to $12,000 depending on how many piers the low side needs.

For a settled slab edge rather than a footing, slabjacking lifts the section back toward level at $500 to $1,500 per section. Once the structure is stable, most doors swing true again on their own; any that still rub can then be adjusted once, permanently. Insist that any quote shows the elevation measurements behind it. Homeowners in community forums describe five-figure pier proposals resting on sticking doors plus roughly 2 inches of measured settlement; the doors were the symptom, the measurements were the evidence, and only the second part justifies piers.

Expect the work itself to be livable. Pier installation happens mostly outside, through small excavations at the footing, and households normally stay in the home while it proceeds. Doors on the repaired side may shift slightly as the lift is applied, which is why the final check matters: walk every door and window with the crew at the end, and get any that still rub adjusted or noted in writing before the last payment.

How much does it cost to fix doors that stick because of settlement?

Budget by the cause, not the door. Stabilising a settled side with piers runs $1,000 to $2,500 per pier in published ranges, with typical whole repairs between $4,000 and $12,000. A settled slab section may instead need slabjacking at $500 to $1,500 per section. Humidity sticking costs nothing structural; an honest evaluation will tell you which bill you actually face.

Will foundation repair make my sticking doors work again?

Usually, yes, if the sticking came from movement. Stabilising the foundation stops the racking, and lifting back toward level often frees doors and windows without anyone touching them. Doors do not always return to perfect, especially in older houses, so a finish carpentry adjustment afterwards is normal. Ask the contractor to check door operation as part of the final walkthrough.

Frequently asked questions

Are sticking windows a stronger warning sign than sticking doors?

Slightly, because windows are less affected by the simple wood swelling that makes wooden doors stick seasonally, and a binding window often reflects frame racking directly. Either symptom alone is weak evidence. The strong signal is a pattern: several doors and windows on one side, worsening over time, joined by diagonal cracks or an uneven floor on that side.

Can sticking doors mean a plumbing leak instead of settlement?

Yes, and it is worth ruling out early. A slow leak under a slab or beside a footing washes fine soil away and drops that section, which racks nearby doors just like clay shrinkage does. Clues include a water bill creeping up, one damp or warm spot on the floor, and sticking that ignores the seasons. A leak detection test settles it.

Do doors stick in new houses too?

They do, and in a new house the cause is usually the build itself: framing lumber drying and shrinking, fill soil under a slab compacting in its first wet seasons, or a door fitted tightly at installation. Most new-build sticking settles down within the first two years. Keep your dated log anyway; it is exactly the record a builder warranty claim needs.

Should I fix my doors before or after foundation repair?

After. Trimming a door or moving a strike plate before the structure is stabilised means doing the job twice, because lifting or even just stopping movement changes the frame geometry again. The exception is a door that no longer closes securely; make it safe with a temporary adjustment, note what you changed, and do the final fitting after repairs settle.

What other inside signs point to foundation settlement?

Look for the cluster rather than any single sign: diagonal cracks above door and window frames, baseboards separating from floors, cabinets pulling away from walls, floors that slope toward the same side, and cracks in tile or brick that line up with the sticking doors. Two or three signs on the same side of the house is the pattern evaluators take seriously.

My door will not close at all anymore. How fast do I need to act?

Treat a door that suddenly stops closing as a prompt problem, not a someday one. Sudden change suggests rapid movement: a void opening, a washout from a broken line, or a footing dropping after extreme weather. Secure the door as best you can, photograph the gaps, and book an evaluation within days rather than waiting for a seasonal log to fill in.
