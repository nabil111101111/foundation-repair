Foundation Repair Companies: The Verification Checklist Before You Sign

This checklist is the one we apply to our own partner companies before they can receive a single match, published in full so you can apply it to any company, including ones that have never heard of us. None of it requires technical knowledge. It requires paperwork, and a willingness to ask for it before you sign rather than after.

The reason a checklist matters here is the pattern homeowners describe again and again: quotes for the same house differing by tens of thousands of dollars, pier counts nobody explained, inspections that turned out to be sales calls, and warranties whose conditions only surfaced when something went wrong. Almost every one of those stories has the same shape. The homeowner found out late what they could have asked for early.

Run the five checks in order. Licence, insurance, the itemised quote, the warranty document, and the independent option. A good company passes all five without drama, because it produces this paperwork on every job anyway. A company that resists any one of them has told you the most important thing it will ever tell you.

Check 1 — Licence: look it up, do not take their word for it

Start with the licence, and start from the honest complication: licensing for foundation work varies by state. Some states licence this trade under a general contractor or specialty licence, some licence the companies at state level, and some states have no specific foundation licence at all. Anyone who tells you a single national rule exists is simplifying. What exists everywhere is a way to check the claim a company makes, and the claim is what you are verifying.

Where a licence applies, ask for the number in writing and enter it in the public lookup on your state licensing board's website. The record should show the same company name, an active status and a future expiry date, and many boards also show disciplinary history. Where no licence applies to the trade, the check shifts rather than disappears: business registration, insurance certificates and warranty documents carry the weight instead, and they are covered next.

What if my state doesn't licence foundation contractors?

Then the licence check becomes a different check, not a skipped one. Licensing rules for this trade vary by state, and some states have no specific foundation licence at all. In those states, verify the company's business registration, its insurance certificates and its warranty terms, and weigh an independent engineer's opinion more heavily, because no regulator has done any screening for you.

How do I verify a licence number?

Ask the company for the licence number in writing, then enter it in the public lookup on your state licensing board's website. The listing should show the same company name, an active status, the licence class and an expiry date in the future. A number that belongs to a different business, a lapsed licence, or a company that will not provide one at all ends the conversation.

Check 2 — Insurance: ask for certificates, not assurances

Foundation work is heavy construction under and around your home. Piers are driven beside footings, soil is excavated, and occasionally things break that nobody planned to break. Insurance decides whose problem that is. A company carrying general liability insurance puts damage to your property on its insurer. A company carrying workers' compensation puts an injured crew member's claim there too. A company carrying neither puts both, potentially, on you.

So ask for a certificate of insurance, which is the standard document insurers issue for exactly this purpose, and check that both policies are named on it and that the dates cover your job. This is routine. Companies send certificates to lenders, permit offices and commercial clients every week. The only companies that find the request strange are the ones you are trying to screen out.

What insurance should a foundation company carry?

Two policies matter: general liability, which covers damage to your home and property during the work, and workers' compensation, which covers the crew if someone is hurt on your land. Without workers' compensation, an injured worker's claim can reach the homeowner. Ask for a certificate of insurance for both, issued by the insurer, and check the dates cover your job.

Can I ask for proof of insurance before signing?

Yes, and a properly insured company expects the question. Certificates of insurance are routine paperwork in construction, routinely sent to customers, lenders and permit offices. Ask before you sign, not after, and treat delay or offence as information. A company that cannot produce a current certificate may be uninsured, between policies, or simply disorganised, and none of those is who you want under your house.

Check 3 — The written itemised quote: what it must show

The quote is where honest companies and sales operations separate. An honest quote is a document you could hand to a competitor, an engineer or a lawyer and have it make sense on its own. A sales quote is a number with a signature line. The difference is itemisation: every part of the job named and priced, so the total can be taken apart and argued with.

Use the table below as the anatomy. When you compare two quotes, lay them side by side and check them line by line rather than total by total. Most of the famous horror-story gaps, the same house quoted at wildly different prices, turn out to be a pier count that differs, drainage included in one and absent in the other, or a lump sum that dissolves the moment you ask what is inside it.

What should a foundation repair quote include?

A usable quote names the pier type and the exact pier count, the price per pier, the depth or bearing method, and separate lines for excavation, permits, engineering and any drainage work. It states whether the goal is lifting the home or stabilising it where it sits. It references the warranty terms. If a line is missing, you cannot compare that quote with another.

How many quotes should I get?

Get at least two, and three if the first two disagree sharply. Foundation quotes for the same house can differ by tens of thousands of dollars because pier counts and scopes differ, and a single quote gives you no way to see which parts are driving the total. Put the quotes side by side line by line before you judge any of them on price.

Check 4 — Warranty: get the terms in writing before work starts

A foundation warranty is only worth what its document says, so the document is the check. Before signing, read for five things: how long the warranty runs, exactly what it covers (usually re-settlement of the piered areas, not the whole house forever), what it excludes, what would void it, and whether it transfers to the next owner when you sell. Transferability matters more than most homeowners expect, because a documented, transferable warranty is evidence a buyer and their lender can actually use.

Two habits protect you here. First, never accept a verbal summary in place of the document; the salesperson's version is a summary of the version that favours the sale. Second, ask who stands behind the warranty. A warranty from a company that disappears is paper. That is not a reason to demand a national brand, it is a reason to weigh how long the company has operated and whether its warranty terms are specific enough to enforce.

What does a transferable warranty actually mean?

It means the warranty passes to the next owner when the house is sold, usually after a transfer process the company defines in writing. That makes it a selling asset, not just protection for you. Before signing, confirm the transfer steps, any deadline after the sale, whether a fee applies, and whether the new owner must register. Get all four answers in the warranty document itself.

What can void a foundation warranty?

Common voiding conditions include later alterations to the repaired area, plumbing leaks left unrepaired under the slab, and major excavation or additions that change the loads on the foundation. Some warranties also restrict who may adjust the system afterwards. The exact conditions live in the company's warranty document, which is why the document, not a salesperson's summary of it, is what you read before signing.

Check 5 — The independent option, and the red flags

The last check is not about the company at all. It is the option of paying for an opinion from someone who sells no repairs. An independent structural engineer will evaluate your foundation and write a scope of what is actually needed, and published sources put that at around $350–$600. It is money well spent when quotes disagree, when the recommended work runs into five figures, or when you are buying or selling and need a document a lender will respect.

And if a company fails the smell test before any of this, believe it. The red flags below are drawn from the stories homeowners tell after bad jobs, and they share a theme: every one of them asks you to stop checking. A company that wants your signature more than your understanding has answered your most important question already.

  • Pressure to sign today, or a discount that expires when the representative leaves
  • No measurements taken: no floor elevations, no crack map, a short visit and a five-figure total
  • Verbal pricing only, or a quote written as one lump sum
  • A pier count nobody will explain against the movement pattern
  • A large deposit, or full payment demanded before work starts
  • A 'lifetime warranty' with no document behind it
  • No licence number, no insurance certificate and no verifiable business address

Should I hire a structural engineer before choosing a company?

Hire one when the stakes or the confusion are high: quotes that disagree by thousands, recommended work in five figures, or a house you are buying or selling. An engineer sells an evaluation, not repairs, and published sources put the cost around $350–$600. The report gives you an independent scope of work that every company's quote can then be measured against.

Frequently asked questions

Two companies quoted me very different prices. How do I choose?

Compare the scopes before the totals. Count the piers in each quote, divide to find the per-pier price, and list what each includes for excavation, permits, engineering and drainage. Most big gaps live in pier count or missing lines, not in generosity. The quote that survives line-by-line questions, and sits inside published ranges, is usually the honest one.

Is a free foundation inspection trustworthy?

A free inspection is useful and a sales call at the same time. The measurements are real: floor elevations, crack mapping, signs of movement. The recommendation that follows is written by a company that profits if you buy the repair. Trust the data, check the recommendation. Insist on the numbers in writing, compare a second quote, and use an engineer when the recommendation runs into five figures.

How do I read online reviews for a foundation company?

Look for specifics and patterns, not star averages. Reviews that describe the problem, the pier count, the timeline and how problems were handled carry information; one-line raves and one-line rants carry almost none. Check whether the company answers complaints and how. Then treat reviews as the weakest layer of evidence, below the licence lookup, insurance certificates and the written quote itself.

Should the cheapest quote win?

Not on its own. The cheapest quote is sometimes efficient and sometimes missing piers, permits or drainage work the others included. Judge it the same way as the expensive one: pier count against the movement pattern, per-pier price against published ranges of $1,000–$2,500, scope lines complete, warranty in writing. A cheap quote that passes those checks is a bargain. One that fails them is a warning.

What deposit is reasonable for foundation work?

Reasonable means small at signing and tied to progress after that. A sound pattern is a modest deposit, staged payments as defined stages of work are completed, and a final payment held until the site is cleaned up and you have walked the finished job. Refuse demands for most or all of the money upfront: a company that needs your cash to start your job is telling you something.

What questions should I ask a foundation company before signing?

Ask who measured the movement and where the readings are. Ask how the pier count was decided, what bearing depth the piers will reach, whether the goal is lift or stabilisation, and what happens if a pier refuses. Ask what the warranty excludes and what would void it. A competent company answers all of these in writing without irritation.
