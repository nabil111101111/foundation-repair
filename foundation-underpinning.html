What underpinning does, in plain terms

A foundation settles when the soil under it can no longer carry the load — clay that shrinks in drought, fill that was never compacted, soil washed out by poor drainage, or a footing poured too shallow. The wall follows the soil down, and the house shows it as cracks, sticking doors and sloping floors. Cosmetic repairs fail because the cause is still moving. Underpinning changes the support itself: new, deep elements are connected to the footing and driven or screwed down until they bear on stable strata, transferring the home's weight past the problem soil entirely.

Once the piers are bearing, the crew can attempt a lift — raising the settled section in small increments toward its original position. Not every home should be lifted the whole way: brittle finishes, additions built after settlement, and plumbing all argue for a partial lift or a stabilise-only job. A good underpinning proposal states plainly whether it is stabilising, lifting, or both, because that choice drives both the price and the risk to your finishes.

What are the types of underpinning?

Underpinning is a family of methods, not one product: steel push piers and helical piers dominate residential work, joined by poured concrete piers, segmented piers, grade beams and micropiles for special cases. The right choice depends on soil depth to bearing, the weight of the structure, access, and whether a lift is planned. Our pier installation page compares push, helical and concrete piers in detail, including where each one fails.

Push piers, helical piers and concrete piers

Push piers are steel tubes driven into the ground using the weight of the house itself as resistance, which makes them self-testing: a pier that cannot be driven to pressure cannot carry the house. They suit heavier structures and situations where the depth to bearing soil is unknown, because sections keep being added until resistance is reached. Helical piers carry steel plates like a screw; they are torqued into the ground and their capacity is read from the installation torque, which makes them verifiable in lighter soils and usable where a structure is too light to drive push piers against. They are also the common choice for new construction on bad soil and for lifting lighter additions.

Poured concrete piers and segmented pressed pilings are the budget end of the market. They can work in shallow, consistent soils, but they depend heavily on installation quality and are harder to verify after the fact. Whichever system a quote proposes, the questions that matter are the same: how deep, bearing on what, verified how, and warrantied by whom.

Which is better for underpinning: push piers or helical piers?

Neither is universally better; they verify differently. Push piers prove capacity by the hydraulic pressure needed to drive them against the house's weight. Helical piers prove it by installation torque correlated to bearing capacity, and they work where a house is too light for push piers. In deep or unknown soils both are proven. Choose based on your soil report and structure weight — and on the installer, because both systems fail when stopped shallow.

How deep do underpinning piers go?

As deep as it takes to reach soil or rock that does not move seasonally — commonly 10 to 30 feet, and occasionally far deeper over fill or soft river soils. The depth is discovered during installation, not guessed beforehand, which is why honest quotes price per pier rather than per foot. Be wary of any proposal that promises a shallow fixed depth in expansive clay; the active clay layer is exactly what the piers must pass through.

Underpinning and leveling costs in 2026

The published bands used across this site put piers at $1,000 to $2,500 each installed, with the pier count set by the length of settled wall and a spacing of roughly five to seven feet. A single settled corner might need four to six piers; a full side of a house, eight to fourteen. That arithmetic is why typical underpinning projects land between $4,000 and $12,000, and why a quote far outside the band deserves line-item questions rather than a signature.

Interior piers cost more than exterior ones because floors must be opened and patched. Lifting adds engineering care but rarely doubles a price. What should never appear is a per-pier price with no stated pier count, depth approach or lift plan — underpinning is sold by the system, and the system details are the quote. Our cost hub shows the same bands applied to worked examples, and the free calculator estimates a range from your wall length and city.

How much does house leveling cost?

House leveling is underpinning plus a lift: the settled part of the home is raised back toward its original elevation on the new piers. Cost follows the pier math — $1,000 to $2,500 per pier, typical projects $4,000 to $12,000 — with the lift itself included in most pier pricing. Quotes that separate a large leveling fee from suspiciously cheap piers are repackaging the same work; compare totals, pier counts and the stated lift plan instead.

Signs you need underpinning — and signs you do not

Underpinning is justified by movement that is structural and ongoing or completed but unacceptable: measured floor slope beyond normal tolerance, cracks that widen season after season, doors racked out of square, a chimney or addition separating from the house. An elevation survey turns those impressions into the numbers a scope is built on, which is why our process starts with an inspection, not a pier catalogue.

Just as important is what does not need underpinning: a single stable hairline crack, cosmetic drywall cracks at door corners in a house with level floors, or settlement that finished decades ago and has not moved since. Stabilising a foundation that is not moving buys nothing. Any company that recommends piers without measurements is guessing with your money; the inspection page explains what to demand instead.

How can I tell if an underpinning recommendation is honest?

Ask for the elevation readings, the crack measurements, and the reasoning that connects them to the proposed pier locations. A legitimate scope can be drawn on your floor plan: these piers, here, because this corner is down this much. If the explanation is a brochure rather than your measurements, get a second inspection — ideally an independent engineer's — before signing. FoundationMatch will match you with another local specialist for exactly that comparison.

Warranties and what to get in writing

Underpinning warranties are a major selling point and a major source of fine print. Ask who backs the warranty — the local installer or a national network — what it covers (further settlement of the piered section is standard; cosmetic damage during lifts is usually excluded), whether it transfers to a buyer, and what maintenance it requires of you, such as drainage upkeep. A lifetime warranty from a company that may not outlive it is worth less than a shorter one from an established installer.

Get the pier count, locations, system type, target depths or verification method, and the lift plan in the contract, plus the before-and-after elevation readings at handover. Those readings are your proof of performance and your baseline if anything moves later. FoundationMatch does not warranty work — your contract is with the installer — which is exactly why this paperwork matters.

What warranty should underpinning come with?

Look for a warranty that covers the piered areas against further settlement for the life of the structure, is backed by an identifiable company or network, and transfers to future owners without a fee or with a small stated one. Read the exclusions before you sign: damage to finishes during lifting, areas outside the pier plan, and problems caused by plumbing leaks or drainage you were told to fix are commonly excluded, sometimes fairly, sometimes as an escape hatch.

Frequently asked questions

How much does foundation underpinning cost in 2026?

Underpinning with piers typically costs $1,000 to $2,500 per pier installed, and most homes need six to twelve piers, putting typical projects at $4,000 to $12,000. The count comes from the elevation survey: piers are spaced roughly five to seven feet apart along the settled section. Full-perimeter jobs and deep or difficult soils cost more; any quote should state the pier count, type and spacing so you can compare it line by line.

Is underpinning the same as piering?

They overlap but are not the same. Underpinning is the goal and the project: strengthening or extending a foundation so it rests on stable soil. Piers are the most common tool used to do it. Underpinning can also be done with concrete piers poured in place, grade beams, or micropiles. When a contractor says underpinning, ask which system they mean and why it suits your soil — the method matters more than the label.

What is the difference between underpinning and house leveling?

Leveling is the goal, not a separate method: a house is leveled by underpinning the settled sections and lifting them back toward their original elevation, then holding them there on piers. Gentle lifts protect finishes; aggressive lifts crack drywall and tile, so experienced crews lift in small increments and stop at the elevation the structure tolerates. Expect the quote to describe the lift plan, not promise a perfectly level house.

Is underpinning a permanent fix?

Properly installed steel piers driven or screwed to bedrock or load-bearing strata are considered a permanent stabilisation: the foundation's weight transfers to soil that does not move with seasons. Warranties commonly run for the life of the structure and transfer to buyers. Permanence depends on reaching competent bearing soil — a pier stopped in the same moving clay that caused the problem is a temporary pier, whatever the invoice calls it.

How long does underpinning take?

Most residential underpinning projects take two to five days: excavation at each pier location, pier installation, the lift itself, then backfill and cleanup. Interior piers, landscaping, decks and concrete in the way add time. You can usually stay in the home throughout. Ask the contractor how they protect landscaping and where excavated soil goes — the answers predict how your yard looks at the end.

How do I know if my foundation needs underpinning?

Common signs are cracks that keep widening or reopening after patching, doors and windows that progressively stick or fall out of square, measurable floor slope that is increasing, and gaps opening between the foundation and the soil or between walls and ceilings. One stable, old crack rarely justifies underpinning. Measurement over time is the honest test, which is why an inspection with elevation readings comes before any underpinning quote on this site.
