Land Subsidence Foundation Damage

Land subsidence foundation damage begins when the ground surface itself loses elevation. Delta soils, the young sediments a river drops at its mouth, are mostly water and loosely packed organic material when they are deposited. Drain that ground, build on it, and the sediments compact: pore water is squeezed out, organic matter decomposes and oxidizes, and the surface quietly sinks, not under one house but under whole neighborhoods at once. The house does not move first. The landscape does, and the house has to follow it down.

That regional scale is what separates subsidence house cracks from ordinary settlement damage. Settlement is one building pressing into its own soil, and it slows down as the soil adjusts. Subsidence is the soil column itself changing, driven by geology and groundwater, and it does not slow down because one homeowner repaired a foundation. Streets, fences, utilities and every house on the block share the motion, which is why the pattern across a neighborhood is the single most useful diagnostic a homeowner has.

This guide explains the mechanism in plain language, how to tell a sinking house from sinking ground, and how the pattern plays out in our two Gulf pilot cities: New Orleans, the extreme American case, built on delta soil with large districts below sea level, and Gulfport, where sandy coastal soils and a high water table produce a milder, different version. It ends with what repair can genuinely do, which is protect the house, and what it cannot, which is stop the ground.

How delta ground sinks: the subsidence mechanism

A river delta is a construction site the river never finishes. Sediment arrives loose and saturated, full of plant matter, and as new layers accumulate the older ones are buried and squeezed. Left alone, the surface roughly holds its level because fresh sediment keeps arriving. Drain and levee the land, as every delta city does, and the replenishment stops while the squeezing continues. The organic fraction makes it worse: exposed to air by drainage, peat and organic silt literally rot away, converting solid soil into gas and water and leaving nothing behind.

Groundwater ties the system together. Pumping water from beneath a district lowers the pressure holding soil grains apart, letting them pack tighter. Even without pumping, a drained water table leaves organic soils above it to oxidize. The result is ground sinking under a house that can continue for generations, at rates set by soil composition rather than by anything the building did. It is the slowest foundation problem in this series of guides, and the least impressed by a single property's repairs.

Is my house sinking or is the ground subsiding?

Check the pattern. If only your house is dropping, especially under its heaviest parts, that is usually settlement of the building into its soil. If the whole street sits lower than it used to, fences and driveways across the neighborhood show the same strain, and flooding keeps getting worse, the ground itself is subsiding. Subsidence is a landscape problem; settlement is a lot problem.

Can subsidence be stopped?

At the scale of one house, no. Regional subsidence is driven by soil and water conditions across a whole district, and no repair on a single lot reverses it. What a homeowner can do is protect the house itself: support the foundation on deeper, stable material where it exists, keep the structure level, and manage water on the lot. Stopping the region sinking is infrastructure work, not foundation repair.

Subsidence versus settlement: two different problems wearing the same cracks

The cracks look alike, which is why the two get confused, but their behaviour diverges. Settlement is worst early in a building's life, or where fill was poorly compacted, and it decelerates: each year moves less than the year before, and a crack monitor shows the damage going quiet. Soil subsidence repair questions start from the opposite observation: movement that continues at a steady background pace for decades, shared by neighbouring properties, and indifferent to whether the house on top was recently repaired.

One practical test is the fence line. Settlement stops at your lot boundary; subsidence does not. If the house across the street, the pavement, and the drainage ditch are all part of the same slow story, you are looking at ground movement. The distinction decides the repair strategy: settlement can be permanently underpinned and finished, while subsidence calls for support plus a maintenance relationship with the house, planned re-leveling included.

What is the difference between subsidence and settlement?

Settlement is a building pressing its soil down or its soil shrinking, usually worst early in a house's life or where fill was poorly compacted, and it slows with time. Subsidence is the ground surface across an area losing elevation, driven by the soil itself changing: organic material decomposing, sediments compacting, water being withdrawn. Settlement slows; subsidence, left alone, continues.

Why do raised houses need re-leveling so often in subsiding areas?

Because the ground keeps moving after the house is fixed. A raised pier-and-beam house is leveled by adjusting its piers and sills, which works until the soil drops further and the piers effectively stand at different heights again. In subsiding districts, periodic re-leveling is normal maintenance, planned the way repainting is, rather than a sign the last job failed.

Subsidence in our pilot cities: New Orleans and Gulfport

New Orleans is the textbook case. Built in the crescent of the Mississippi on delta soil, the city drained its backswamps and sank with them: research by Tulane geographer Richard Campanella, based on LIDAR measurement, puts roughly 49 percent of the urbanized tri-parish area below sea level. The districts that sank deepest are the ones drained latest and built on the most organic soil: Lakeview, Gentilly, New Orleans East and the Lower Ninth Ward, where organic-soil subsidence and post-Katrina settlement define the local foundation story. The original city had it right: the French Quarter and Garden District sit on the natural levee ridge, several feet above sea level, and largely escaped both flooding and the worst sinking.

The housing stock encodes the adaptation. Historic raised pier-and-beam and raised-basement homes across Uptown, Mid-City, the Marigny and Bywater, and Algiers Point can be re-leveled as the ground falls, which is why pier settlement and leveling is a distinct, routine service category there rather than an emergency trade. Gulfport, our second Gulf city, is the contrast: sandy coastal soils and a high water table near the shore and Bayou Bernard, where settlement is more about sand, water and post-Katrina rebuild stock than deep organic subsidence, and where raised beach cottages and inland 1960s ranch slabs follow two different foundation profiles in one small market.

Why is so much of New Orleans below sea level?

The city was built on young delta soils that compact and sink once drained and built on. Research by Tulane geographer Richard Campanella, based on LIDAR data, puts roughly 49 percent of the urbanized tri-parish area below sea level. The natural levee ridge along the river stayed high, which is why the French Quarter and Garden District sit on the city's best ground.

What foundation problems come with living below sea level?

The list starts with drainage: your lot cannot shed water downhill, so moisture management is constant, and any ground loss shows up as worsening street and yard flooding. Add foundations chasing sinking soil, raised homes drifting out of level, and slabs cracking as the ground beneath them consolidates unevenly. Below-sea-level ownership means budgeting for leveling and drainage as recurring maintenance, not one-time repairs.

Repairing on subsiding ground: protect the house, respect the ground

Soil subsidence repair starts by changing what carries the house. Steel piers, at $1,000 to $2,500 each installed in published ranges, transfer the structure's load through the sinking surface soils to deeper, firmer material, and a typical structural repair runs $4,000 to $12,000. Raised houses are often handled the other way: piers and sills adjusted or supplemented so the house can be re-leveled now and again later. Slabs on badly consolidating ground may need support plus void filling where soil has pulled away beneath them. What all the honest versions share is an admission: the ground will keep moving, so the system is chosen to tolerate or be adjusted to that movement.

The wasted spending in subsidence country is cosmetic certainty. Repeatedly patching cracks without support buys the same crack at intervals. Jacking a slab level without addressing the consolidating soil beneath books a second appointment with the same problem. And aggressive lot drainage, tempting in a wet city, can backfire on organic soils by drying and oxidizing them faster. The right conversation with any contractor there starts with one question: what is your repair standing on, and what happens when the ground under it drops further?

How do you repair a foundation on subsiding soil?

The usual answer is to stop asking the surface soil to carry the house. Steel piers, at $1,000 to $2,500 each installed in published ranges, transfer the load to deeper, firmer material, and a typical structural repair runs $4,000 to $12,000. Raised houses are often re-leveled on adjusted or added piers instead. Cosmetic patching without deep support only hides a moving house.

Can I do anything to slow subsidence on my own lot?

A little, at the margins. Keep drainage from stripping soil away, avoid pumping groundwater, fix plumbing leaks quickly, and think twice before aggressive dewatering, because drying organic delta soil makes it oxidize and shrink faster. None of this stops a district from subsiding. It keeps your lot from sinking faster than your neighbors' and protects the foundation work you pay for.

Frequently asked questions

What are the signs of subsidence rather than ordinary settlement?

Look beyond your own walls. Subsidence shows up as a neighborhood pattern: several houses cracking the same way, streets and driveways dropping relative to curbs, gates and fences leaning together, and flooding that worsens over years. Cracks from subsidence also keep progressing instead of stabilizing. A single house with one old, stable crack is telling a different, smaller story.

Does subsidence damage a house evenly across the whole lot?

No, and the unevenness is the damage. Ground rarely sinks at one tidy rate: soil is thicker in one spot, wetter in another, more organic under the old garden. So one side of the house drops faster, floors tilt, and rigid materials like brick and tile crack where the twist concentrates. Uniform sinking would mostly threaten drainage; differential sinking threatens structure.

Should I buy a house in a subsiding area?

Plenty of people do, happily, in cities like New Orleans where the culture and the housing stock are worth it. Go in with open eyes: get the foundation evaluated, ask about the leveling history, budget recurring leveling and drainage work as ownership costs, and understand local flood exposure. A subsiding district is a manageable condition, not an automatic deal-breaker.

Why does my raised house feel bouncy or out of level years after a repair?

Because leveling is a position, not a permanent state, on sinking ground. Piers and sills hold the house where they were set while the soil continues to consolidate below, so the relationship between house and ground drifts again. Bouncy floors can also mean individual piers have settled or timber has aged. A fresh level survey tells you which piers moved and by how much.

Will filling the low spots in my yard fix subsidence?

No. Fill raises the surface, and for drainage and appearances that can be worth doing, but it does nothing about the soil column compacting underneath, and heavy new fill can even add load that speeds consolidation in soft ground. Fill is landscaping. Foundation support and district drainage are the structural answers, and they work at completely different depths.

How fast does the ground subside?

Slowly enough to ignore for years and steadily enough to matter over a mortgage. Subsidence is measured in the accumulation: the crack that widens a little each year, the door adjusted twice in a decade, the street that floods where it never used to. There is no universal rate we can honestly quote for your lot; a surveyor comparing elevations over time gives the only number that counts.
