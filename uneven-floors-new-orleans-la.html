Uneven Floors in New Orleans, LA

A slightly uneven floor in an old house is common and often harmless; a floor that is getting worse is not. As a working threshold, many older homes sit comfortably with up to about 1 inch of slope across 20 feet. A slope over about 2 inches, a slope concentrated inside one room, or a dip that keeps deepening crosses into evaluation territory, whatever the age of the house.

In New Orleans, the details are local: homes here sit on Schriever soil over deltaic soils and ongoing subsidence, and 73.8% of the housing stock predates 1980. Measure the slope before you judge it

Under New Orleans, the sampled soil is Schriever — 81.9% topsoil clay with a high shrink–swell rating (LEP 19.2%). Before the word foundation enters your head, put a number on the floor. Slope is a total drop over a distance, and the distance matters as much as the drop: 1 inch of fall across a 30-foot house is gentle, while 1 inch inside a single 10-foot room is steep. A long level, a laser level, or a straight board with a level on top gives you the two numbers you need. Photograph the level reading or write the numbers on the wall inside a closet, somewhere they will survive until the next check.

Published guidance for older housing treats roughly 1 inch over 20 feet as the upper edge of the range many homes live with, especially when it has not changed in years. Beyond about 2 inches over that span, doors and drains misbehave, buyers notice, and the slope usually has an active cause. In between, the trend decides: a stable inch and a half is a trait of the house; a growing inch and a half is a problem.

Evaluators lean on the same thresholds because finishes start confessing at predictable amounts. Around an inch of differential movement, door frames rack enough to stick and brittle finishes start to crack; around two inches, slopes become visible to the eye along baseboards and countertops. Your measurements therefore do double duty: they tell you whether to act, and they set the baseline that proves whether a repair actually worked.

How much floor slope is normal?

Up to about 1 inch of slope over 20 feet is common in older homes and is often stable and harmless, particularly when it has not changed in years. More than about 2 inches over the same span, a slope packed into a single room, or any slope that is still increasing calls for a professional evaluation rather than more monitoring.

Is a sloping floor dangerous?

Rarely in the short term, and it should not be ignored either. A sloping floor is almost never a collapse warning by itself; houses telegraph serious structural failure through cracked and displaced framing first. The real costs of leaving an active slope are progressive: doors and drains stop working, finishes crack, resale takes a hit, and the eventual repair gets larger.

Sagging is not sloping: two problems that feel the same

73.8% of New Orleans homes were built before 1980, so most uneven floors work here happens on older footings. Roll a marble across the room and it reports two different stories, and they take different repairs. If the whole floor is a flat plane tilted toward one wall, that is slope, and slope comes from the ground: one side of the foundation settled lower than the other. If the middle of the room dips like a shallow bowl while the edges stay level, that is sag, and sag usually lives in the framing, not the footing. Furniture quietly reports the same split: a bookcase that leans with the room suggests tilt, while a table that rocks on a hump in mid-room suggests sag.

Sag is the crawl-space and pier-and-beam story. Joists weaken from moisture and rot, a beam loses support when a wood post or a masonry pier deteriorates, or the original joists were undersized for their span and have crept downward over decades. A bounce or sponginess underfoot points the same way, because settled footings make floors tilt; they do not make floors trampoline.

Plenty of houses have both at once, which is where quick diagnoses go wrong. A house can tilt toward a settling corner and sag in a hallway where the crawl space stayed wet for years. Treating only the tilt leaves the hallway bouncing; treating only the sag leaves the corner travelling. The room-by-room measurements in the self-check section are what separate the two layers before anyone writes a scope of work.

Is it sagging floor joists or the foundation?

Check where the low spot sits. A dip in the middle of a room with level edges points to joists, beams, or crawl-space supports. A steady tilt from one side of the house to the other points to the foundation. A look inside the crawl space usually settles it: rotted or deflected joists and failed posts are visible from the access hatch.

Why does my floor bounce or feel spongy in one area?

Bounce means the structure under that spot is flexing: joists that span too far for their size, a beam whose support has softened or dropped, or subfloor damaged by moisture. It is a framing problem far more often than a footing problem. Have the crawl space inspected, because spongy areas tend to sit directly over the moisture source that caused them.

What makes floors go uneven

New Orleans averages 68.7 inches of rain a year — moisture management is part of any lasting fix. Framing causes start with water. A crawl space that stays damp rots sill plates and joist ends, feeds wood-destroying insects, and softens the soil under interior support posts until they settle. Loads added later can finish the job: a library wall, a stone hearth, a cast-iron tub, or a load-bearing wall removed during a remodel can overload joists that were perfectly adequate for the original house.

Ground causes mirror the soil map of the country. Expansive clay in Shreveport and Abilene drops a slab edge in drought and lifts it after spring rain, so the slope itself changes with the season. New Orleans subsidence lowers whole blocks slowly and steadily. A karst void near Lexington, or washout in Gulfport's sandy soil, can drop one corner within a single season. Frost in Iowa and Wisconsin tilts shallow porch footings and, occasionally, more than the porch.

Additions deserve a paragraph of their own. A porch converted to a room, a garage conversion, or a later extension often stands on shallower or differently built footings than the main house, so the two parts move on different schedules and the floor between them develops a hinge line. The slope in those rooms points at the joint, and the fix usually belongs to the addition's footing rather than to the house as a whole.

Interior supports fail quietly, and they are worth understanding because they are the cheapest cause on this page to fix. Many pier-and-beam and crawl-space homes carry their middle on posts or masonry piers that were set on shallow pads, sometimes just blocks on soil. Those pads settle into softened ground at their own pace, each one dropping the beam it carries by a little. The floor above develops a wave pattern, low over the failed supports and level over the sound ones, which is exactly the pattern the string test in the next section is built to find.

How do you fix an uneven floor in an old house?

Match the fix to the layer that failed. Sagging framing is repaired in the crawl space with sistered joists, new beams, and adjustable steel support jacks, typically a $3,000 to $10,000 project. A tilted foundation is stabilised with piers at $1,000 to $2,500 each. Raising anything happens slowly, in small steps, so finishes and plumbing are not damaged by the correction itself.

Can jacking a floor back up cause new damage?

Yes, if it is done fast or aimed at the wrong target. Lifting a floor quickly can crack plaster and tile, rack doors into sticking, and stress rigid plumbing lines, which is why experienced crews lift in small increments over days. Sometimes the right answer is partial correction: stabilise the floor and remove most of the slope instead of forcing an old frame to dead level.

Check it yourself: marble, level, and string

Under New Orleans, the sampled soil is Schriever — 81.9% topsoil clay with a high shrink–swell rating (LEP 19.2%). The marble test is famous and genuinely useful, within limits. A marble or golf ball released near the centre of the room rolls toward the low side and shows you the direction of slope in seconds. What it cannot show is the amount, and a marble rolls happily on a slope no engineer would repair. Direction first, then measure.

For the amount, lay a long level across the floor, or a straight board with a level on top, and measure the gap under the high end. Repeat along the room's other axis and write it down as inches over feet, such as 1.25 inches over 16 feet. The string test finds sag: stretch a taut line wall to wall just above the floorboards and measure the gap at the middle, where sag hides.

Keep the room-by-room record together in one place, because its second use is commercial. Two quotes for levelling the same house can differ by thousands of dollars, and the measurements let you see whether both contractors are even describing the same problem. A quote that names target elevations room by room is a different instrument from a quote that just says relevel, and your dated numbers are how you tell them apart before signing.

  • Measure the total drop and the distance with a long level, a straight board, or a laser level
  • Write the slope as inches over feet, dated, room by room
  • Stretch a string line wall to wall, just above the floor, to check for mid-room sag
  • Look into the crawl space from the hatch for failed posts, rot, or standing water
  • Remeasure each season; change over time matters more than any single reading

How do I measure floor slope in inches per foot?

Measure the total drop over a known distance, then divide. If one wall sits 1.5 inches lower than the other across a 15-foot room, the slope is 1.5 inches over 15 feet, or about 1/10 inch per foot. Half an inch per foot would be very steep for a house floor; concern usually starts well below that, at total drops over 2 inches.

What it costs to level an uneven floor

73.8% of New Orleans homes were built before 1980, so most uneven floors work here happens on older footings. Prices split cleanly by layer. Crawl-space structural work, meaning support jacks, replacement beams, and sistered joists, runs $3,000 to $10,000 in published ranges for a typical project, and it fixes sag rather than tilt. A tilted footing is stabilised with piers at $1,000 to $2,500 each, with typical structural repairs totalling $4,000 to $12,000. A settled concrete slab section, such as a porch or garage floor, can often be lifted by slabjacking at $500 to $1,500 per section instead.

The trap in this market is the bargain relevel: a very cheap quote, an hour of work, and a floor that still slants, a pattern homeowners describe with real frustration in community forums. Levelling is verifiable work, so make the quote prove it before you pay: the support method, the number and location of supports or piers, and the target elevations in writing, with a shared understanding that old floors are often stabilised and improved rather than returned to perfectly level.

Sequence the spending around the diagnosis. If a leak or drainage failure is feeding the problem, that repair comes first and is sometimes the only urgent one; structure can be stabilised after the soil stops changing. If the cause is long-finished settlement or tired framing, there is rarely a reason to rush, and there is real value in collecting two or three itemised quotes, because scopes in this trade vary more than prices do.

Why did a cheap relevel job leave my floor still slanted?

Usually because the job lifted or shimmed a symptom instead of adding real support, or because it levelled one room against a house that tilts as a whole. Ask what was installed: proper work leaves steel jacks, new beams, or piers you can inspect, plus before-and-after elevation numbers. Without those, you paid for effort rather than correction, and fresh measurements will show it.

Should an old floor be lifted back to perfectly level?

Often not all the way. A frame that settled decades ago has finishes, plumbing, and masonry that all adjusted to the settled shape, and forcing it back to dead level can crack more than it cures. Most engineers aim to stabilise first, then recover whatever slope can be recovered safely. Agree the target elevation in writing before work starts.

Uneven Floors in New Orleans: local questions

What soil conditions affect uneven floors in New Orleans?

New Orleans homes sit mostly on Schriever soil, with 81.9% clay in the topsoil and a shrink–swell rating of 19.2% (high). Crews plan depth and spacing around that soil behaviour, because a fix that ignores how the local ground moves with moisture will not hold. Ask any contractor bidding on your home to explain, in writing, how their plan accounts for this soil — a good one answers without hesitating.

Are older New Orleans homes more likely to need uneven floors?

Yes — 73.8% of New Orleans's housing was built before 1980 and 50.4% before 1960. Those homes have older footing designs and fifty-plus years of soil movement behind them, so they make up most serious repair work. Age alone is not a diagnosis, though: what matters is measured movement, so an older home with stable readings may need nothing at all.

How does New Orleans's rainfall affect uneven floors?

New Orleans averages 68.7 inches of rain a year, peaking in Aug. Wet months swell the soil and load walls with water pressure; dry months shrink it away from footings. That cycle is why timing and drainage are part of any durable repair plan here — fixing the structure without managing the water that moves the soil usually means the problem comes back.

Frequently asked questions

Can uneven floors be fixed?

Yes, in nearly all cases. Sagging framing is repaired with joists, beams, and support jacks; tilted foundations are stabilised with piers; settled slab sections can be lifted by slabjacking. What varies is the end point: a floor can almost always be made stable and much more level, but a very old floor may not return to perfectly flat without damaging the finishes around it.

Do uneven floors hurt resale value?

A documented, stable slope in an old house is often a negotiation point rather than a deal-breaker, while an unexplained or worsening slope frightens buyers and their inspectors. Measurements help you: a dated record showing the floor has not moved, or an engineer's letter after a repair, converts a vague fear into a known quantity during a sale.

Is it safe to live in a house with sloping floors?

In the large majority of cases, yes. Sloping floors usually reflect slow soil or framing movement measured in fractions of an inch per year, not imminent failure. Safety becomes a real question when change is fast, a floor feels soft or springy underfoot, or framing is visibly cracked or crushed; those signs deserve a prompt inspection rather than monitoring.

Will leveling the floor fix my sticking doors and wall cracks too?

Often it improves them, because the same movement that tilted the floor racked the door frames and tore the drywall. Stopping the movement is the main win; lifting can close small cracks and free sticking doors, but hairline cracks in finishes usually still need cosmetic patching afterwards, and a door that was trimmed to fit the old shape may need refitting.

How long does floor leveling take?

Crawl-space support work typically takes a few days for an average house, and pier installation under one side is similar once access is dug. The schedule stretches when lifting must happen in stages, which is deliberate: crews raise a little, let the structure adjust, and measure again. Anyone promising to perfectly level a badly tilted house in a single day is skipping that care.

The marble rolls fast in my living room. How worried should I be?

Less than the marble suggests. A marble accelerates on slopes too small to matter, so treat a fast roll as a prompt to measure, not a verdict. Measure the drop over the room's width: under about 1 inch over 20 feet with no other symptoms is a monitoring case; over 2 inches, or doors and cracks joining in, is an evaluation case.
