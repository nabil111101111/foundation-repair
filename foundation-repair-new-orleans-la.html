Why foundations move in New Orleans

New Orleans is sinking, slowly and measurably, and that fact shapes every foundation decision in the city. The urbanized area sits on delta sediment rich in organic material. As that material dries and decomposes, it compacts, and the surface drops. A Tulane study by geographer Richard Campanella, using LIDAR data, found roughly 49 percent of the urbanized tri-parish area sits below sea level, with parts of Lakeview and New Orleans East several feet under.

Elevation is destiny here. The oldest neighborhoods were built on the natural levee the river deposited along its banks, high ground that stayed comparatively dry even in 2005. Neighborhoods developed later, on drained backswamp and marsh, started lower and keep subsiding faster. That is why one street can need leveling every generation while a street a few blocks away, on the levee ridge, barely moves.

The city's drainage system feeds the process. Because so much of New Orleans must be pumped dry, groundwater levels swing with pumping and rainfall, and soil near the canals and in the lowest lots dries, shrinks, and settles unevenly. Add the roughly fifty levee and floodwall failures that flooded about 80 percent of the city after Katrina's landfall on August 29, 2005, and you get a housing stock where foundation history is as much a record of water as of construction.

Signs of foundation problems in New Orleans

Foundation damage starts small, then stops being small. These are the warning signs New Orleans homeowners notice first:

  • Stair-step cracks in brick or block. Cracks following mortar joints in a stair pattern usually mean one section is dropping faster than the rest.
  • Cracks in foundation walls. Horizontal cracks suggest soil pressure; vertical cracks wider at one end suggest settlement. Both deserve measurement, not patching.
  • Doors and windows that stick. When a frame racks out of square, doors rub, latch poorly, or swing on their own — often seasonally at first.
  • Uneven or sloping floors. Floors that tilt, bounce, or separate from baseboards point to support being lost underneath.
  • Cracks in drywall and ceilings. Diagonal cracks from door and window corners — and frames pulling away from walls — echo the movement outside.
  • Water in the basement or crawl space. Water follows cracks, and persistent moisture weakens soil, so leaks and movement arrive together.

In New Orleans, look for brick separating from the frame, porches pulling away from the house, and doors that have been re-hung repeatedly — all common subsidence tells.

One sign alone can be innocent. Two or more together — especially if worsening or changing with the seasons — usually mean the foundation is moving and should be measured.

Foundation repair methods used in New Orleans

The right method depends on what is moving and what the soil below your home can support:

Steel push piers

Galvanized steel piers are driven hydraulically, using the weight of the home, until they reach stable strata below the moving soil. Brackets transfer the load, and the footing can often be lifted back toward level.

Helical piers

Helical piers are screwed into the ground like large steel screws, and installation torque verifies capacity as they go. They suit lighter structures and low-vibration sites.

Slabjacking (mudjacking)

A settled slab is raised by pumping grout or foam through small holes beneath it, filling voids and lifting it back to grade. Fast and inexpensive — but if the soil keeps moving, piers are the better answer.

Drainage & grading correction

Many foundation problems are water problems first. Regrading soil to slope away from the house, extending downspouts, and adding drainage keeps moisture consistent at the footing.

Repair methods that fit New Orleans housing stock

On raised homes in Uptown, Mid-City, the Marigny and Bywater, and Algiers Point, leveling is the core service. Crews jack the structure in small lifts, reset or replace the brick or concrete piers it stands on, and swap out rotted sills and beams. Because the work happens from the crawl space, it is less invasive than slab repair, and the same visit usually handles the moisture damage that weakened the wood in the first place.

Slab homes in Gentilly and New Orleans East get a different treatment. Steel or helical piers at $1,000-$2,500 each carry settled sections down to firmer strata, and foam lifting at $1,000-$2,500 can fill voids and raise interior slab sections that piers cannot reach. In a subsiding city, the goal is often stabilization at a consistent elevation rather than a perfect original level, and a good scope says which one you are paying for.

If my raised home was leveled before, why is it sloping again?

Usually the ground, not a bad pier. New Orleans sits on organic soil that keeps compacting, so the grade around a home can drop even when the piers themselves are sound. Crews check pier condition and soil level together. If the piers are undersized for today's soil, adding properly placed supports is what makes the next leveling last longer.

How deep do piers go in New Orleans soil?

In New Orleans, piers pass through the soft organic layer to reach the firmer sand and clay strata that actually carry load. Installers verify capacity by drive pressure or torque as each pier goes in, so depth follows the soil, not a fixed number. On some lots that means surprisingly deep piers, which is normal here and not an upsell by itself.

How a leveling or pier job runs in New Orleans

Everything starts with an elevation survey. In a city where the ground itself is dropping, guessing from cracks alone leads to the wrong scope, so a proper inspection measures floor levels room by room and inspects every pier and sill from the crawl space. You should get those readings in writing, along with a pier-by-pier plan, before any work is quoted.

The work itself is methodical rather than dramatic. Raised homes are lifted a fraction at a time so brick, plaster, and plumbing are not shocked, and crews pause to let the structure settle onto its new support. Work in Orleans Parish is permitted through the city-parish, and scheduling should account for the rainy season and Carnival alike. Most owners stay in the home throughout; expect noise under the floor, not a move-out.

Do I need to move out during a leveling job?

No, not for a typical leveling or pier job. Crews work underneath the house and around the perimeter, and utilities stay connected. You may be asked to clear the crawl-space access and move items off a section of floor for a day. Only major structural reframing, which is rare in a leveling scope, would change that answer.

What paperwork should I get before approving work on my New Orleans home?

Ask for three things: the elevation survey, the pier placement plan, and the warranty document. The survey proves where the house actually stands. The placement plan shows exactly what you are buying, pier by pier. The warranty matters more in New Orleans than in most cities, because ongoing subsidence makes transferable coverage a real part of the home's value.

Foundation repair questions from New Orleans homeowners

Is my New Orleans home sinking because of subsidence?

Quite possibly. Much of New Orleans sits on organic delta soils that compact, shrink, and slowly decompose, so the ground itself drops over time — a process called subsidence. When one part of a lot subsides faster than another, the foundation tilts or cracks. An inspection with elevation measurements can confirm how much movement is soil-wide versus specific to your footings.

Can a raised pier-and-beam home be leveled?

Yes — raised homes are actually the most straightforward to re-level. Crews access the crawl space, add or shim piers, and replace rotted sills or beams where needed. Costs are usually lower than slab piering because there is no concrete to break through. The bigger question in New Orleans is moisture: rotten wood should be addressed at the same time as leveling.

What does foundation repair cost in New Orleans?

Re-leveling a raised home often starts lower than slab work, while slab piering follows typical ranges: piers around $1,000-$2,500 each installed and a typical structural job near $4,000-$12,000. Widespread subsidence can push pier counts up. Get an elevation survey and a written, pier-by-pier quote — flat guesses are unreliable in New Orleans soil.

Will leveling fix my sloping floors permanently?

It fixes the current elevation, and piers driven to stable strata resist future movement far better than the original shallow footings. But New Orleans soil keeps subsiding citywide, so a transferable lifetime warranty matters here more than almost anywhere. Ask exactly what the warranty covers and whether it transfers to the next owner when you sell.

How does being below sea level affect my foundation?

Because much of New Orleans drains by pumping, soil near canals and low lots dries and shrinks more aggressively, and groundwater management is uneven from block to block. That is why two houses on the same street can settle very differently. Keeping your lot's drainage consistent — no standing water, no bone-dry soil at the footing — slows the cycle between repairs.

How much does it cost to level a raised house in New Orleans?

Most leveling jobs on raised New Orleans homes total $4,000-$12,000, with individual piers at $1,000-$2,500 installed where new support is needed. A cottage that needs shimming and a few sill repairs prices at the low end. Deep settlement, rotted beams, or several failed piers push it higher. Elevation readings, not square footage, set the real scope.

My porch is pulling away from the house. What does that mean?

A porch that pulls away usually means the porch piers have settled faster than the house, or the house has moved and the porch has not. The repair re-supports the porch with new or reset piers and reconnects it to the structure. Because porches are lighter than the house, the fix is often smaller than a full leveling, but it should be measured, not eyeballed.

When should I have my foundation checked after a hurricane or major storm?

Book an inspection once the ground has drained, typically a few weeks after a major storm. New Orleans soil keeps moving as it dries, so readings taken in standing water can mislead. Compare the post-storm elevation survey against any earlier measurements you have. Movement that shows up after one storm usually traces to drainage or saturation, not a sudden structural failure.

Is foundation damage covered by flood insurance or homeowners insurance in New Orleans?

They cover different things, and neither usually pays for subsidence. Homeowners insurance excludes earth movement and long-term settling. Flood insurance covers flood damage to the structure, not gradual sinking of the ground under it. A sudden, covered event, such as a burst pipe undermining a footing, is the rare exception. Most New Orleans repairs are paid out of pocket.

How long does shoring or leveling work take on a raised home?

Most raised-home leveling jobs take two to four days. Crews work from the crawl space, jacking in small increments, replacing failed piers and sills as they go. Larger homes, extensive rot, or brickwork that needs to be re-supported can stretch the job to a week. Slab piering follows a similar timeline, though interior access points add cleanup time.

Should I still buy a New Orleans home that has had foundation work?

Yes, and in New Orleans they check specific things. Buyers should get elevation readings, look under the house for rotted sills and undersized piers, and ask the seller for any leveling history and warranty paperwork. Subsidence is a known, manageable fact of owning here. A home with documented, warrantied repairs is often a safer buy than one with none.
