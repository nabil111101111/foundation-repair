Frost Heave Foundation Damage

A frost heave foundation problem starts with a simple fact: water expands when it freezes. When soil moisture freezes in place it grows in volume, and as the freezing front pushes deeper, liquid water migrates toward it and freezes in stacked layers, called ice lenses, that can jack the ground upward with enormous force. Anything resting in that freezing zone, a shallow footing, a garage slab, a porch, gets lifted. Anything resting below it, on soil that never freezes, is left alone.

Cold-climate foundations therefore fail in two distinct ways that are often confused. Freeze-thaw foundation damage is damage to the material itself: water inside concrete, brick, stone and mortar freezing and thawing until faces pop off and joints crumble. Frost heave is damage by movement: the soil underneath changing height with the seasons. A house in Des Moines or Madison can suffer either, or both at once, and the fixes are different enough that telling them apart is the first job.

This guide covers how heave lifts a foundation, why winter foundation cracks so often declare themselves in spring, and how the pattern runs in our three northern pilot cities: Des Moines, Cedar Rapids and Madison. One honesty note up front: frost depth varies so much by locality that this guide deliberately gives no numbers for it. Footings belong below the local frost line; your local code states where that line is.

How frost heave lifts a foundation

Ice lenses are the engine. Soil is not frozen solid in one pass; a freezing front moves down through it, and water in the unfrozen soil below is drawn up toward the front, where it freezes onto the growing lens. As long as water keeps arriving, the lens keeps thickening, and the soil surface above it rises, carrying whatever sits on it. Silty soils feed lenses best, which is why heave is so uneven: one stretch of footing over lens-friendly soil rises, the next stretch barely moves, and the foundation between them is twisted.

The second mechanism works sideways and upward at once: adfreeze. Frozen soil bonds hard to the surface of a foundation wall or footing, so when the surrounding soil heaves, it can drag the wall up with it, even where the footing itself sits deep. That grip explains damage that puzzles homeowners, a wall lifted or shifted while its base stayed put. Both mechanisms share one requirement: water. Dry soil has little to freeze and heaves weakly, which is why drainage runs through every prevention strategy in this guide.

Can frost heave lift a foundation?

Yes. When water in soil freezes it expands, and as the freezing front deepens, extra water migrates toward it and freezes in layers called ice lenses that keep growing. Those lenses can lift soil, slabs and shallow footings with enormous force, easily enough to raise a garage floor or porch. Deep footings escape it because they sit below the freezing zone.

Why do shallow footings heave when deep ones do not?

Heave only happens inside the zone that actually freezes. A footing poured below the local frost line rests on soil that stays unfrozen all winter, so there is nothing under it to expand. A shallow footing, common under garages, porches and older additions, sits inside the freezing zone, where ice lenses form directly beneath it and push it upward.

Freeze-thaw damage: when the wall itself is the casualty

Freeze-thaw foundation damage works on the building material, not the ground. Water soaks into concrete pores, mortar joints and the face of brick or stone, freezes, expands, and micro-fractures the material; the next thaw lets water in deeper, and the cycle repeats dozens of times in a single northern winter. The visible results are spalling faces, crumbling mortar, and cracks that grow from the surface inward. Old masonry suffers most, especially where its original soft lime mortar was repointed with hard modern cement that traps moisture inside the wall.

Heave and freeze-thaw also conspire at the basement wall. Saturated backfill freezing against a wall presses inward all winter, adding to the sideways soil pressure that bows basement walls over the years, and the wall that bows a little more each winter is often being worked on by both processes at once. Keeping water out of the backfill, with grading, gutters and drainage, is therefore both a freeze-thaw defence and an anti-bowing defence.

Why do foundation cracks get worse in spring?

Spring is when winter's hidden work shows itself. Soil thaws from the top down, meltwater saturates the ground, and walls that frozen soil pushed or gripped all winter settle back unevenly. Cracks opened by heave widen as support softens, and saturated backfill presses harder on basement walls. A crack that grows every spring is reporting active seasonal movement, not old damage.

What does frost do to a basement wall?

Frozen soil grips a basement wall hard enough to drag it upward slightly, and saturated soil freezing against the wall also pushes inward, adding to the sideways pressure that bows walls over time. The grip, called adfreeze, is why a wall can heave at the top while its footing stays put. Good drainage behind the wall is the main defence.

Frost country in our pilot cities: Des Moines, Cedar Rapids and Madison

Des Moines pairs freeze-thaw with old masonry. Neighbourhoods like Sherman Hill, Drake and Beaverdale hold stone and brick foundations 80 to 150 years old, built with lime mortar that breathes and flexes a little. The local failure pattern is the repointing trap: a hard Portland cement repoint traps moisture in the wall, and freeze-thaw then spalls the masonry face season after season. Cold Iowa winters cycling over glacial soils stress these foundations twice over, once through the ground and once through the wall.

Cedar Rapids brings the long-winter basement version: cold, snowy winters over a housing stock with a heavy pre-1960 concentration in Wellington Heights, Oakhill Jackson and around downtown, where older basements meet both heave and freeze-thaw. Madison adds water to the cold. Its isthmus and lakefront stock, Tenney-Lapham, Marquette, Atwood, sits on narrow, high-water-table land between Lakes Mendota and Monona, and local contractors flag settling and freeze-thaw protection as the standing concerns for the city's Victorian, Greek Revival and Craftsman houses. Wet soil plus deep freeze is precisely the recipe both heave mechanisms order.

Can frost heave lift a garage slab and then settle back?

Yes, and garage slabs are the classic victim because they are thin, lightly loaded and poured shallow. Ice forming beneath a slab lifts it in winter, sometimes enough to jam the door or crack the slab at the control joints. In spring it usually drops back, but not always evenly, and repeated cycles can leave it permanently tilted or cracked.

How do I tell frost heave from settlement?

Heave rises in winter and often eases in spring; settlement only goes down and never recovers. Heave also tends to lift the shallowest, lightest parts first: porch slabs, garage floors, additions. Settlement follows load and soil failure, and it progresses in any season. A problem that breathes with the calendar is frost; one that only sinks is settlement.

Fixes that work against frost, and spending that does not

Because heave needs water, the first fix is always drainage: extend downspouts well away from the house, grade soil so meltwater leaves instead of ponding, and keep saturated backfill off basement walls. The second defence is insulation. Rigid insulation placed around shallow footings and slabs holds the ground's own heat in and shrinks the frozen zone, a standard technique for protecting porches, garages and additions that cannot practically be dug to full footing depth. The third, for footings that have ratcheted out of level over years of lifting and uneven dropping, is underpinning: steel piers at $1,000 to $2,500 each installed in published ranges, with a typical structural repair at $4,000 to $12,000.

Freeze-thaw material damage has its own rule: repair like with like. Old lime-mortar masonry needs a compatible, softer mortar, not the hardest mix on the shelf, and coatings that seal moisture inside a wall make spalling worse. The wasted spending in frost country is timing-blind: sealing cracks in midwinter while the soil is still heaved, levelling a slab before thaw completes, or injecting a crack that is still moving with the seasons. Measure in spring, on thawed ground, and most frost problems become smaller, clearer and cheaper to fix correctly.

Does frost heave go away in spring?

Mostly. As the soil thaws, ice lenses melt and the ground usually settles back close to its winter position, which is why heaved slabs and sticky doors often sort themselves out by late spring. The catch is the word usually: soil does not always return evenly, and each season of lifting and dropping can leave a little permanent displacement behind.

How do I protect shallow footings from frost?

Keep water away from them first, because dry soil heaves far less than wet soil: extend downspouts, grade the surface away, and fix drainage against the wall. Insulation board placed around shallow footings is the other standard defence, holding ground heat in and shrinking the frozen zone. Footings that have already ratcheted out of level may need underpinning to stable depth.

Frequently asked questions

How deep does frost go in my state?

It varies enough that no single number in this guide would be honest for your house. Frost penetration depends on latitude, elevation, soil type and snow cover, and local building codes set footing depths below the expected frost line for exactly that reason. Your city or county building department publishes the required footing depth for your address, and that local figure is the one that matters.

Are winter foundation cracks an emergency?

Rarely. Most winter cracks are the visible end of slow seasonal movement and can wait for a spring evaluation. The exceptions are a crack that opens fast, a wall that leans or bows noticeably further, or water pouring in during thaw. Mark crack ends with a dated pencil line in winter; growth past the marks by spring tells you the movement is active.

Why is the face of my brick or stone foundation crumbling?

That crumbling, called spalling, happens when water inside masonry freezes and pops the face off, cycle after cycle. It is worst on old stone and brick foundations whose original soft lime mortar was repointed with hard modern cement, which traps moisture inside the masonry. The fix is compatible mortar and better drainage, not harder coatings, which usually speed the damage.

Should I repair a heaved slab in the middle of winter?

Almost never. A slab lifted by frost is still sitting on ice, and its true position will not be known until the ground thaws completely. Leveling or replacing it midwinter means working against a moving target. Document the heave, keep water away, and schedule evaluation for after thaw, when measurements mean something and most slabs have dropped back toward level.

Does snow piled against the house hurt the foundation?

It can, in two ways. Deep snow banked against a wall delivers a slow flood of meltwater straight at the footing in spring. The bigger risk is drainage: meltwater should leave the lot, not pond against the house. Keep a clear path for meltwater away from the wall, keep downspouts extended, and never let spring runoff pool against the foundation.

What is the difference between frost heave and freeze-thaw cracking?

Frost heave is movement: frozen soil lifting the foundation or slab. Freeze-thaw cracking is material damage: water inside concrete, brick or mortar freezing and breaking the material itself. A house can suffer either without the other, though in cold climates they often arrive together. The distinction matters because heave is fixed with depth, insulation and drainage, while freeze-thaw damage is repaired in the masonry itself.
