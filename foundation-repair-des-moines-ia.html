Why foundations move in Des Moines

Des Moines sits where two rivers meet, and the ground near them remembers it. The Des Moines and Raccoon Rivers cut through the city, Saylorville Lake holds floodwater back upstream, and the Great Flood of 1993 put water across whole neighborhoods along both banks. Homes on the low ground near the rivers sit on soil that was saturated to depth, then dried, then soaked again. Soil with that history does not settle evenly, and river-adjacent houses are the ones most likely to show slow, one-sided settlement years after the water is gone.

Away from the rivers, the vulnerable stock is the oldest masonry. Sherman Hill, Drake, and Beaverdale homes stand on stone and brick foundations 80 to 150 years old, laid with lime mortar that stays slightly flexible as the ground cycles through wet springs and frozen winters. The damage often starts with a well-meant repair: repointing those joints with hard Portland cement. Portland is stronger than the old masonry around it, so freeze-thaw movement the soft mortar once absorbed now cracks the brick and stone faces instead, flaking them off in layers. On an older Des Moines home, the mortar is part of the structure, and matching it is part of the repair.

Signs of foundation problems in Des Moines

Foundation damage starts small, then stops being small. These are the warning signs Des Moines homeowners notice first:

  • Stair-step cracks in brick or block. Cracks following mortar joints in a stair pattern usually mean one section is dropping faster than the rest.
  • Cracks in foundation walls. Horizontal cracks suggest soil pressure; vertical cracks wider at one end suggest settlement. Both deserve measurement, not patching.
  • Doors and windows that stick. When a frame racks out of square, doors rub, latch poorly, or swing on their own — often seasonally at first.
  • Uneven or sloping floors. Floors that tilt, bounce, or separate from baseboards point to support being lost underneath.
  • Cracks in drywall and ceilings. Diagonal cracks from door and window corners — and frames pulling away from walls — echo the movement outside.
  • Water in the basement or crawl space. Water follows cracks, and persistent moisture weakens soil, so leaks and movement arrive together.

In Des Moines, cracks that open in winter and partly close in summer are a classic sign that clay movement and freeze-thaw cycles are shifting your foundation with the seasons.

One sign alone can be innocent. Two or more together — especially if worsening or changing with the seasons — usually mean the foundation is moving and should be measured.

Foundation repair methods used in Des Moines

The right method depends on what is moving and what the soil below your home can support:

Steel push piers

Galvanized steel piers are driven hydraulically, using the weight of the home, until they reach stable strata below the moving soil. Brackets transfer the load, and the footing can often be lifted back toward level.

Helical piers

Helical piers are screwed into the ground like large steel screws, and installation torque verifies capacity as they go. They suit lighter structures and low-vibration sites.

Slabjacking (mudjacking)

A settled slab is raised by pumping grout or foam through small holes beneath it, filling voids and lifting it back to grade. Fast and inexpensive — but if the soil keeps moving, piers are the better answer.

Drainage & grading correction

Many foundation problems are water problems first. Regrading soil to slope away from the house, extending downspouts, and adding drainage keeps moisture consistent at the footing.

Repair methods that fit Des Moines' older housing stock

Most Des Moines foundation work falls into three buckets: underpinning settled footings with steel piers, stabilizing basement walls that soil pressure is pushing inward, and managing the water that drives both problems. On newer poured-wall homes the work looks like it does anywhere. On the older stock — stone, brick, and block foundations in the pre-war neighborhoods — the method has to respect what the wall is made of. A stone foundation cannot be treated like a poured concrete wall.

Masonry repair on these homes starts with mortar. Failed lime-mortar joints get raked out and repointed with a mortar matched to the original: softer than the brick or stone, so the joint takes the freeze-thaw movement instead of the masonry. Structural movement is handled on its own track: piers under settled footings, anchors or carbon-fiber straps on walls moving inward, and interior drainage where groundwater is part of the load on the wall.

Can a Sherman Hill stone foundation be repaired, or does it have to be replaced?

Most stone foundations can be repaired in place, and full replacement is rare. Loose or missing stones are reset, failed joints are repointed with mortar matched to the original lime mix, and settled sections can be underpinned with piers. Replacement enters the conversation only when large sections have collapsed or bulged beyond stabilizing. An inspection that includes the mortar condition, not just the crack pattern, gives you the answer.

What mortar should be used to repoint an old Des Moines brick or stone foundation?

A lime-rich mortar matched to the original, softer than the masonry it joins. Hard Portland cement is the wrong choice on 80-to-150-year-old Des Moines foundations: it traps moisture and forces freeze-thaw movement into the brick and stone faces, which spall and flake. A mason who works on historic homes will test or match the existing mortar rather than defaulting to the strongest bag on the truck.

What a foundation repair in Des Moines actually looks like, step by step

The work starts with measurement, not digging. A proper inspection maps floor elevations across the house, measures how far walls have moved, and checks the mortar, drainage, and soil contact outside. From that, the contractor writes an itemised quote: how many piers, where, what wall work, what drainage, each priced separately. If a quote arrives as one lump number with no measurements behind it, you do not have enough to compare it against anyone else's.

Installation on a basement home usually runs from outside excavations at the footing, a few feet wide at each pier location, with the soil and plantings put back when the lift or stabilization is done. Interior work, when the plan calls for it, means small openings in the basement floor at each bracket. Crews verify each pier as it goes in: push piers by the pressure it takes to drive them, helical piers by torque. You should get those readings, plus before-and-after elevations, in writing.

Can I live in my Des Moines home while piers are being installed?

Yes, almost always. Pier installation is outside work at the footing or through small openings in a basement floor, and families routinely stay in the house through it. Expect noise and vibration while each pier is driven or screwed to load-bearing soil, and keep kids and pets clear of the open excavations. Water and power interruptions are rare and usually brief if they happen at all.

How do I know the piers under my home were installed to the right depth?

Each pier produces a reading as it is installed, and that reading is your proof. Push piers are driven until resistance pressure shows they have reached stable, load-bearing soil; helical piers are screwed in and the installation torque is converted to a capacity figure. Ask for the pressure or torque log for every pier, plus the final elevation readings across your floors, before you make the final payment.

Foundation repair questions from Des Moines homeowners

Why do so many Des Moines homes develop foundation cracks in winter?

Central Iowa clay absorbs water in the fall, then freezes and expands as temperatures drop. That frost pressure pushes against basement walls and shallow footings, and when the soil thaws and dries it shrinks back. After years of this swell-shrink cycle, walls crack and footings shift. Cracks that widen every winter are a sign the movement is active, not cosmetic.

My basement wall is bowing inward. Is that serious?

Yes — a bowing wall means soil pressure is winning against the wall, which is common in Des Moines clay. A slight bow can often be stabilized with wall anchors or carbon-fiber straps, but a wall that has moved more than a couple of inches may need excavation and piers. Get it measured during a free inspection before it progresses.

How much does foundation repair cost in Des Moines?

Most Des Moines homeowners pay in the typical national range: piers run about $1,000-$2,500 each installed, a typical structural repair lands around $4,000-$12,000, and slabjacking runs $500-$1,500 per section. Your exact price depends on pier count, access, and whether drainage work is needed. A written quote after inspection is the only reliable number.

Will installing piers tear up my yard or driveway?

Usually not much. Pier brackets are installed through small excavations at the footing, typically a few feet wide, and crews restore the soil and landscaping when they finish. Interior piers may require small openings in a basement floor. A good contractor will walk you through exactly where digging happens before work starts.

Does homeowners insurance cover foundation repair in Iowa?

In most cases, no. Policies generally exclude settlement, soil movement, and wear over time, though sudden events like a burst pipe that undermines a footing may be partly covered. Because insurance rarely helps, most Des Moines homeowners compare quotes carefully — which is why a free inspection and written estimate matter before committing.

Why do foundation repair quotes in Des Moines vary so much from company to company?

Quotes vary mainly because pier counts and scope vary, not because the work itself changes. Piers run $1,000 to $2,500 each installed in published national ranges, so a plan calling for eight piers and a plan calling for fourteen land far apart. One quote may include interior drainage while another leaves it out. Itemised quotes, compared line by line, expose the difference.

My Beaverdale brick home has spalling bricks on the foundation. What is happening?

Spalling means the faces of your bricks or stones are flaking off, and on Beaverdale's 1920s and 30s brick homes the usual cause is a hard Portland cement repoint done years earlier. The rigid mortar traps moisture in the masonry, and Des Moines freeze-thaw cycles pop the faces off. The fix is repointing with a softer, lime-matched mortar, plus checking whether the wall itself has moved.

What is the best time of year to get foundation work done in Des Moines?

Late spring through fall is the practical window, when the ground is thawed and excavation is straightforward. The smarter timing move is diagnostic: have cracks measured in late winter or early spring, when freeze-thaw movement is at its most visible, so the inspection catches the problem at its worst rather than its best. Book early, because Des Moines contractors' calendars fill once the thaw hits.

Will homeowners insurance pay to fix a bowing basement wall in Des Moines?

Almost never. A bowing wall is caused by soil pressure and freeze-thaw movement building over years, and Iowa policies treat earth movement and gradual settling as excluded wear, not a covered accident. Insurance sometimes responds when a sudden event, like a burst pipe, undermines a footing. For a bowing wall, plan on paying out of pocket, and put the money into stabilization before the wall needs rebuilding.

How long does a typical foundation repair take on a Des Moines home?

Most Des Moines pier and wall jobs take two to five days on site. A small job — a few piers on one wall — can finish in two days, while a full perimeter underpinning with wall anchors and interior drainage runs closer to a week. The inspection and quote come first, and scheduling depends on the season: spring calendars book out fastest after the thaw.

Should I repair foundation problems before selling my Des Moines home?

Usually, yes — or price the home as if the buyer will. Findings in an inspection report are among the most common reasons Des Moines deals get renegotiated or fall apart, and buyers discount by more than the repair costs because the scope feels unknown. A completed repair with a transferable warranty and the elevation paperwork removes the unknown. At minimum, get an itemised quote so the negotiation uses numbers.
