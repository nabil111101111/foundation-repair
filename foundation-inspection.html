What a foundation inspection actually checks

Outside, the inspector walks the full perimeter looking at the foundation wall itself: crack locations, widths and patterns, spalling or bowing, previous repairs, and how the ground, grading and drainage sit against the house. Crack pattern matters more than crack existence — a vertical hairline in a poured wall is usually shrinkage, while a stair-step crack through block joints, a horizontal crack, or a crack wider at one end is telling a movement story.

Inside, the inspection turns to symptoms and measurement. Floors are checked for slope with a level or elevation survey, doors and windows are tested for sticking and racking, and finishes are read like instruments: drywall cracks radiating from door corners, tile cracks, gaps where walls meet ceilings. In the basement or crawl space the inspector looks for the causes — water entry, soil washout, rotted or undersized supports, pier condition — because fixing a symptom without its cause is how repairs fail.

Do inspectors measure floor levels, or just look around?

It should, in any inspection worth the name. Floor elevation measurement — with a water level, laser, or digital altimeter — converts a feeling of slope into numbers: how many inches of differential across the house, and where the low and high points sit. Those measurements drive the repair scope and later prove whether a lift worked. If an inspector will not measure, ask why before accepting their conclusions.

What should I prepare before the inspection?

Bring any previous inspection reports, repair invoices and warranty papers, disclosure documents if you are buying, and photos showing when cracks or sticking first appeared. Note which doors stick, which rooms feel sloped, and whether symptoms change with seasons or rain. Fifteen minutes of your observations saves the inspector guesswork and often sharpens the diagnosis — seasonal movement, for example, points at clay soil behaviour rather than a one-time settlement event.

Free contractor inspection vs a structural engineer

Most foundation repair companies inspect for free because the visit doubles as their quoting opportunity. A good company inspection is genuinely useful: experienced eyes, measurements, and a priced scope of work. Its limit is structural, not personal — the inspector's employer profits only if you buy a repair, so the incentive leans toward finding one. That is why the standard advice from homeowner forums, realtors and engineers alike is to get more than one quote and to treat a single free inspection as a starting point, not a verdict.

An independent structural engineer sells only the diagnosis. For roughly $350 to $600 you get a signed report with measured findings, probable causes, and recommended remedies — and no repair to sell you. Engineers are the right call when the stakes are high: a purchase or sale riding on the outcome, a dispute, an insurance claim, or quotes from contractors that disagree wildly. FoundationMatch arranges the free local inspection; when an engineer's report is the smarter step, we say so, and several pages on this site explain how to use one.

When is a paid engineer worth it over a free inspection?

Choose the engineer when the decision is expensive or contested: buying or selling, quotes that disagree by thousands, an insurance claim, or any situation where you need a document a third party must accept. Choose the free contractor inspection when you want a problem confirmed and priced and intend to gather two or three competing quotes. The two are not enemies — many homeowners start free, then hire an engineer to referee before signing anything large.

How is a foundation inspection different from a home inspection?

A foundation inspection by a repair specialist is a focused examination of the foundation and related symptoms, usually free when tied to a quote. A general home inspection is a broad survey of the whole house — roof, electrical, plumbing, HVAC — by a licensed home inspector, who will typically flag foundation concerns and recommend a specialist rather than diagnose them. For foundation questions specifically, the specialist or the engineer gives you measurements and causes; the home inspector gives you a referral.

What a good inspection report includes

Whether free or paid, judge the inspection by its paper. A useful report shows a floor elevation map or the measured differentials, a crack map with widths, photographs, the likely cause stated in plain language, and a recommended scope separated into must-do structural work and optional or cosmetic items. Vague reports that skip measurements and jump straight to a pier count are sales documents wearing a lab coat.

The report should also say what does not need fixing. Honest inspections routinely conclude that cracks are cosmetic, movement is old and stable, or monitoring is the right prescription — and a company willing to say 'no repair needed' in writing has earned more trust than one whose every inspection ends in a five-figure proposal. Keep the report with your home records; it anchors future comparisons, resale disclosures and warranty claims.

Will I get the inspection results in writing?

Yes — ask before the visit, not after. Through FoundationMatch, the local specialist's inspection includes a written summary of findings and any recommended scope with pricing, at no charge. An independent engineer's deliverable is a formal signed report. If any inspector hesitates to commit findings to paper, treat the visit as a sales call and weigh its conclusions accordingly; the written record is what protects you in quotes, claims and resale.

Reading the findings: the four usual verdicts

Most inspections end in one of four places. One: no structural problem — cracks are shrinkage or age, floors are within normal tolerance, and the prescription is monitoring and cosmetic patching. Two: water management first — the foundation is sound but drainage, grading or a wet crawl space is creating symptoms that will become structural if ignored. Three: localised repair — a section of wall, a corner, or a few piers where movement is concentrated. Four: broader stabilisation — settlement across a side or the whole footprint that justifies underpinning. The verdict should follow from the measurements on the report; if it does not, ask the inspector to walk you through the numbers.

Costs attach to verdicts, and our cost hub breaks down the published bands by method so you can sanity-check the quote that follows an inspection: piers at $1,000 to $2,500 each installed, typical structural repairs at $4,000 to $12,000, and the smaller fixes — crack injection, drainage, slab lifting — priced in their own ranges.

What should I do right after the inspection?

Get the findings in writing, then decide in this order: fix water problems first if any were found, because they drive movement; gather at least one competing quote for any structural scope over a few thousand dollars; and consider an engineer's report if the quotes disagree about the cause. There is rarely a same-week emergency in foundation work — active water entry and sudden new movement are the exceptions that justify moving fast.

Frequently asked questions

How much does a foundation inspection cost in 2026?

A contractor's foundation inspection booked through a repair company is commonly free when it leads to a repair quote, while an independent structural engineer's evaluation typically costs $350 to $600. You pay for the engineer's independence: they sell a report, not a repair. If the findings will drive a five-figure decision, a real-estate dispute, or an insurance claim, the paid independent report is usually the better buy.

How long does a foundation inspection take?

A thorough inspection takes 45 minutes to two hours for a typical house. The inspector measures floor elevations, maps cracks inside and out, checks doors, windows and finishes for distortion, and examines the crawl space or basement where most causes live. Rushed ten-minute walk-throughs that end in a repair pitch are sales calls, not inspections; ask beforehand what the visit includes and what you receive in writing.

When should I get a foundation inspection?

Book an inspection when you see new or widening cracks, doors that suddenly stick, floors that feel sloped, gaps at ceilings or cabinets, or water entering a basement or crawl space — and before buying or selling a home with any of those signs. Seasonal hairline cracks that never change are usually cosmetic. When in doubt, measurements settle it: an inspection now is cheaper than a repair scoped on guesses later.

Does insurance pay for a foundation inspection?

Usually not for the inspection itself, because insurers treat inspections as maintenance and diagnosis rather than a covered loss. If the inspection uncovers damage from a covered event — a burst pipe is the common example — the resulting repair may be claimable, and the inspection report becomes key evidence. Our insurance and financing guide explains what foundation claims typically cover and how to document them.

What do I get after a foundation inspection?

Through a repair company, the findings often arrive as a verbal summary plus a repair quote, which is useful but not neutral. An independent engineer gives you a signed, stamped report with measurements, causes and recommended remedies that you can use with any contractor, a buyer, a seller or an insurer. If you plan to act on the findings beyond one company's quote, the written independent report is the document worth having.

Does getting an inspection mean I have to do the repair?

No. An inspection identifies problems, likely causes and options; it is not a repair and it does not oblige you to hire anyone, including the company that inspected. Through FoundationMatch the inspection visit itself is arranged free with a local specialist, and any repair quote that follows is yours to accept, decline or take to another company for a second price. That separation is the point of getting inspected before getting sold.
