Spring Foundation Inspection Checklist: What to Check After the Thaw

The best time to check your foundation after winter is early spring, as the ground thaws and before heavy spring rains arrive. Winter does its damage quietly: frozen soil pushes against walls, snowmelt raises the water table, and freeze-thaw cycles work cracks a little wider each time temperatures swing across freezing. A spring foundation inspection checklist run catches that damage while it is still seasonal movement, before another winter turns it into structural movement.

In Des Moines, the recurring high-water pattern is exactly this: spring snowmelt plus heavy rain pushing the Des Moines and Raccoon Rivers up, with river-bottom neighborhoods carrying the flood risk. Cedar Rapids winters are long, cold, and snowy, so the thaw releases months of accumulated snow into soil that is still partly frozen and cannot drain it. In both cities, the spring home foundation check is a local ritual for a reason: it is the one moment when winter's effects are fully visible and summer repairs can still be scheduled calmly.

Work through the outside first, then the inside, photographing as you go. The whole check takes about an hour and needs nothing more than a flashlight, a tape measure, your phone, and a garden hose for the sump test.

Why Spring Reveals What Winter Did

Frost and water do the damage; spring is when you can read it. Soil that froze saturated expands and presses on basement walls, and as it thaws it softens, settles, and sometimes pulls away. Snowmelt adds a second load: meltwater runs off still-frozen ground instead of soaking in, pools against foundations, and raises the local water table for days or weeks. That is why thaw basement leaks appear in homes that were dry in January. The water was always going to come; spring is when it arrives all at once.

The inspection logic follows from that. You are comparing the house against itself: cracks versus last year's marks, doors versus how they closed in fall, grading versus where the water actually went during the melt. A single spring check tells you the state of things. Two or three years of dated checks tell you whether the foundation is moving, which is the only question that really decides between monitoring and repair.

Why does my basement leak in spring but not in winter?

Basements leak in spring because snowmelt and spring rain saturate the soil while the ground below is still frozen and cannot drain it, so water builds up against the foundation and pushes through cracks, joints, and the wall-floor seam. The raised water table adds pressure from below as well. It is a drainage-load problem first, which is why grading and downspouts matter more than interior coatings.

Which spring foundation cracks should I actually worry about?

Hairline cracks under about a sixteenth of an inch that have not changed are usually cosmetic shrinkage or minor seasonal flex, and monitoring them with dated marks is enough. Worry about cracks that are wider, growing year over year, horizontal rather than vertical, offset so one side sits proud of the other, or leaking. Those patterns mean soil pressure or settlement is actively moving the wall or footing.

The Exterior Checklist: Walk the Perimeter First

Start outside, because most spring foundation problems begin as water-management problems you can see from the yard. Work slowly around the whole house in order, phone in hand. Photograph every crack with a coin or tape measure in frame for scale, every place soil has settled against the wall, and anything that changed since fall. Dated, scaled photos are what an engineer or an insurer will ask for later, and they turn a vague worry into a measurable record.

Two items on this walk matter most. The spring grading drainage check: soil should slope away from the foundation on every side, and winter frost heave plus settling often reverse that slope in spots, creating a trough that funnels meltwater straight to the wall. And the drainage path check: every downspout should discharge several feet from the foundation into ground that continues falling away, not into a settled trench beside the footing.

  • Start at the highest corner of the lot and walk the full perimeter slowly, in one direction, so nothing gets skipped.
  • Photograph every foundation crack with a coin or tape in frame; note which are new versus last year.
  • Check grading on all four sides: soil should fall away from the wall, with no settled troughs holding meltwater against the foundation.
  • Clear gutters of winter debris and confirm every downspout discharges several feet out, onto ground that keeps sloping away.
  • Look for soil pulled away from the foundation, washed-out spots, and mulch or beds piled against the wall above the foundation line.
  • Inspect porches, steps, and garage slabs for new tilt, separation from the house, or fresh cracking.
  • Check window wells for standing water and debris, and confirm covers sit properly.
  • Photograph the whole wall of each side from a distance, so next year's check can be compared frame by frame.

What is the first thing to fix if meltwater is pooling at my foundation?

Gutters and downspouts come first. A winter's worth of debris blocks gutters, meltwater spills over the edge, and it lands in a trench right against the foundation, exactly where spring soil is softest. Clear the gutters, then follow each downspout to its outlet and confirm the water exits several feet from the wall onto ground that slopes away. This one fix prevents more spring leaks than any product.

How do I monitor a crack between spring checks?

Place a pencil mark across the crack at its widest point, write the date beside it on painter's tape, and photograph it with a ruler in frame. If the wall moves, the mark will offset or the crack will grow past it. Repeat every spring. A crack monitor gauge does the same job more precisely and costs little, but dated marks and photos are enough for most homes.

The Interior Checklist: Basement, Cracks, and the Sump Pump

Inside, start at the walls. Read every basement or crawl-space wall for new or changed cracks, paying attention to pattern: vertical cracks that taper are usually shrinkage or minor settlement, horizontal cracks suggest soil pressure, and stair-step cracks through mortar joints in block walls point to footing movement. Compare against last spring's photos and marks. Run a hand along the wall for dampness, look for efflorescence, the white mineral crust that proves water has been moving through the concrete or block, and check the wall-floor joint for seepage lines or silt.

Finish with the mechanical check. The sump pump spring check takes five minutes: lift the float or pour a bucket of water into the pit and confirm the pump starts, discharges outside, and shuts off. Check that the discharge line is not frozen, blocked, or dumping right beside the foundation. Then stand back and read the floor: new sloping, a door that suddenly sticks after closing fine all winter, or fresh cracks radiating from corners all suggest the ground under the house moved during the freeze.

How do I test my sump pump in spring?

Do a sump pump spring check before melt season peaks: open the pit, clear debris, pour in water to lift the float, and watch the pump start, discharge outside, and shut off cleanly. Confirm the discharge exits away from the foundation and the line is not frozen or crushed. A pump that hums without moving water, or never starts, gets replaced now, not during the storm.

What does the white crust on my basement wall mean?

Efflorescence, the white crusty deposit on basement walls, means water has been moving through the concrete or block and evaporating inside, leaving minerals behind. It is not itself damaging, but in spring it maps exactly where meltwater is entering. Photograph its extent: a growing patch year over year means the water path is growing too, and the fix belongs outside with grading and drainage, not with a scrub brush.

What to Do With What You Find

Sort findings into three groups. Monitor: hairline cracks that match last year's marks, minor grading dips you can correct with soil and a rake, gutters and downspouts doing their job after a cleaning. Fix yourself or cheaply: regrading, extending downspouts, replacing a failed sump pump, sealing a leaking crack that is not moving. Escalate: cracks that grew, a horizontal or offset crack, a wall that feels out of plumb, doors newly sticking in several rooms, or water arriving through the floor rather than the walls.

For the escalate group, call a structural engineer before a foundation repair company when the pattern is unclear. The engineer's paid evaluation, typically $350 to $600, measures elevations and names the cause; the repair company's free inspection usually arrives with a quote attached. If piers or wall stabilization are genuinely needed, published pricing runs $1,000 to $2,500 per pier installed, with typical structural repairs at $4,000 to $12,000. Your spring checklist, with its dated photos and crack marks, is what lets you test any quote against the facts.

When should I call a professional instead of monitoring?

Call a professional when a crack is horizontal, offset, leaking, or measurably wider than last spring, when a wall bows or leans, when several doors suddenly stick, or when water enters through the floor. Choose an independent structural engineer first if you need a diagnosis you can trust, and a repair company when the cause is already clear and you are collecting itemized quotes for a defined fix.

Should I seal the cracks I find during the spring check?

Yes, if the crack is old, dry, and not moving; sealing keeps water and pests out and costs little. Sealing a crack that is still moving just hides your best monitoring tool: the seal splits and you learn nothing. Mark and measure first, confirm stability across a season, then seal cosmetic cracks. Moving cracks get stabilized first and sealed as part of that repair.

Frequently asked questions

What is the single most effective spring flooding prevention step?

Spring basement flooding prevention starts outside: regrade soil that settled toward the house over winter, clear gutters, and extend downspouts well past the foundation. Inside, test the sump pump before melt season peaks. Most spring water in basements is meltwater and rain arriving faster than the soil can drain, so moving roof water away early prevents more problems than any interior product applied later.

What does a spring snowmelt water table mean for my basement?

The snowmelt water table is the seasonal rise in groundwater as melting snow and spring rain saturate the soil faster than it can drain downward. Around a foundation, that rising water presses against walls and finds cracks, joints, and the floor seam. It is why basements that stay dry all winter leak for a few weeks in spring, then dry out again once the ground drains.

How fast do foundation cracks get worse in spring?

How soon foundation cracks get worse in spring varies: active cracks can widen across a single freeze-thaw season, while stable hairline cracks sit unchanged for a decade. The trend across two or three springs is what matters, which is why dated photos and pencil marks each spring beat memory. A crack that has grown every spring you have measured it is a moving crack, whatever its current width.

My cracks open in winter and close in spring. Is that normal?

Cracks that open in winter and partly close in spring usually reflect seasonal movement: frozen, saturated soil pushing in winter, then soil settling back as it thaws and drains. That cycle is common in Iowa and Wisconsin and is worth monitoring rather than panic. It becomes a repair question when the crack closes back less each year, or starts leaking, meaning the wall is not returning to position.

How much does a foundation inspection cost in spring?

An independent structural engineer evaluation typically runs about $350 to $600 in most markets, and a foundation inspection booked in spring should ideally happen before you finish the basement or list the house, not after a buyer raises the issue. Repair companies often inspect free but sell repairs; the engineer's paid opinion is the one with no work attached to it.
