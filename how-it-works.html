How Foundation Repair Works, Step by Step

Foundation repair works in four stages: a measured inspection, an itemised written quote, the repair itself, and warranty paperwork at the end. Most pier jobs take one to three days on site, and you can live in the house throughout. The stages matter because every later argument, about price, pier count or warranty, is settled by what was measured and written down in the first two.

Homeowners comparing quotes often see wildly different totals for the same house. That gap usually comes from scope, not magic: one company measured floor elevations and proposed nine piers, another eyeballed the cracks and proposed twenty. Knowing how foundation repair works, and what the foundation inspection process should actually produce, lets you spot which quote is a diagnosis and which is a sales pitch.

Step 1: The inspection, where everything is measured

A proper foundation inspection starts with a floor elevation survey. The inspector moves room to room with a measuring instrument, commonly a water manometer or electronic level, and records how high or low each point sits relative to a reference point. Those readings become a contour map of your floor, showing where the foundation has dropped, by how much, and whether the pattern points to one corner, one wall or the centre of the house.

Next comes crack mapping. The inspector records each significant crack, its width, direction and whether it has offset, plus related signs: doors that bind, gaps at ceilings, separations around windows. Outside, they check grading, gutter discharge, soil moisture near the footing and any plumbing-leak clues. What happens during a foundation inspection should always end with drawings, not just an opinion: an elevation map, a crack map and a proposed pier layout that you keep.

Keep those documents even if you do not hire that company. Elevation readings taken on the same reference system let a second company, or an independent engineer, verify the first findings instead of starting over, and a dated elevation map becomes your baseline for judging whether movement continues. If an inspector will not leave measurements behind, or offers only a pier count and a total, treat the visit as what it was: the opening of a sale, not an inspection. A real inspection produces paper.

  • Floor elevation map with readings, not just a verbal summary
  • Pier layout diagram: type, count and location of every pier
  • Per-pier price and total, itemised line by line
  • Lift plan: how much recovery is targeted, and what stays as stabilisation only
  • Warranty document: type, duration, transfer terms and exclusions
  • What is excluded: landscaping, paving, interior cosmetic work
  • Payment schedule tied to stages, never the full amount up front

What should a foundation repair quote include?

A foundation repair quote should include a pier diagram showing each pier's location, the pier type and count, a per-pier price, the lift plan, and the warranty terms in writing. It should also state what is excluded, such as landscaping restoration or interior cosmetic repairs, plus a payment schedule. If any of those are missing, ask for them before signing anything.

Can you live in the house during foundation repair?

Yes, in most cases you can live in the house during foundation repair. Crews work from outside at the footing line or from a crawl space, and daily life continues around them. Expect noise during excavation and lifting, short water or power interruptions if lines are moved, and dust near work areas. Full evacuation is rare and usually tied to major interior structural work.

Steps 2 to 4: Scheduling, the repair day by day, and the lift

Once you sign, the company schedules the work, orders materials and, where your city requires it, pulls a permit. On day one the crew exposes the footing at each pier location, usually by hand digging or small excavation, and sets the brackets that tie the piers to the foundation. Piers are then driven or screwed down to load-bearing soil or rock, one at a time, with pressure or torque readings logged as they go.

The lift comes last and moves slowly. Hydraulic jacks raise the settled section in small increments while the crew watches doors, windows and crack gaps respond. Recovery is partial by design: the goal is a stable foundation, and pushing for perfect original level can crack plumbing, walls and finishes. After lifting, the crew backfills, regrades the soil against the house, reconnects anything that was moved and walks the site with you before warranty paperwork is signed.

Use that walkthrough properly. Check that excavations are backfilled and compacted, that soil slopes away from the house again, that any sprinkler lines, cables or AC connections disturbed during digging are reinstated, and that doors and windows the lift was meant to free actually move. Ask for the post-repair elevation readings, taken the same way as the originals, and file them with the contract. If something on the site is unfinished, note it on the walkthrough sheet before the final payment, because a written reservation is far easier to enforce than a memory of a conversation.

How long does a pier repair take?

A typical pier repair takes one to three working days on site. Small jobs, one corner with four to six piers, can finish in a day; a full side or a whole-house job with fifteen or more piers runs longer. Weather, soil depth to stable ground, and how much lift is attempted stretch the schedule more than pier count alone.

Will lifting my house close the cracks in my walls?

Lifting closes some cracks, but it does not restore walls to new condition. As the foundation recovers, gaps at doors and ceilings often narrow and sticking doors usually free up. Drywall, plaster and brick cracks that remain are cosmetic repairs, normally handled separately after the foundation settles into its corrected position. Any quote promising perfect cosmetic results from a lift is overselling what hydraulics can do.

The lift itself: stabilise first, recover second

Videos of a house lifting in real time make it look dramatic, but a real lift moves fractions of an inch at a time, pausing between increments. The crew is chasing two different goals, and it helps to know which one your quote promises. Stabilisation stops further movement by transferring the load to deep, stable soil. Recovery goes further and raises the house back toward its original position. Stabilisation is nearly always achievable; full recovery is not, especially where movement has happened slowly over decades.

A good crew will tell you in advance which sections they expect to recover and which they only plan to hold. They will also warn you that lifting can open new hairline cracks in brittle finishes and can stress rigid plumbing runs. That honesty matters more than the headline lift figure, because over-lifting a section that wanted to stay put is how repair jobs create new damage while fixing the old.

After the lift, expect a settling-in period. Small hairline cracks can appear in paint and drywall joints over the following weeks as the frame adjusts to its corrected position, and flooring may feel different underfoot. This is one reason experienced companies suggest waiting before doing cosmetic repairs: patching and repainting too early often means doing the same work twice. If doors that freed up start binding again within the warranty term, or cracks reopen along the same lines, that is not settling-in, it is renewed movement, and it belongs in a warranty call with your before-and-after elevation maps in hand.

Can I repair just one side of the house now and the rest later?

Yes, phasing a repair is common and often sensible. Many homeowners stabilise the settled corner or wall first, then monitor the rest. Ask for the full elevation map at inspection so later phases build on the first, and get the phased plan in writing. What you should avoid is lifting one section aggressively while an adjoining settled section stays put, which can twist the frame.

Warranty decoder: lifetime, limited and transferable, in plain terms

Foundation repair warranty language is where paperwork matters most. A lifetime warranty usually means the life of the structure as the company defines it, not your lifetime, and it covers only the piers or sections that company installed. A limited warranty covers a set number of years. Neither type covers new movement in areas that were not repaired, and neither covers damage caused by things outside the repair, such as a long-term plumbing leak under the slab.

Transferable is the word that matters when you sell. A transferable foundation warranty passes to the next owner, but many companies require the transfer to be registered in writing within a set window after the sale, sometimes with a fee. Homeowners have been told at resale time that using a different company for new work would void an existing warranty. Whether that is true depends entirely on the written terms: if a condition is not in the document, it is not a term. Keep the warranty certificate with your house records.

If the repaired area moves again, the claims process normally starts with a re-inspection: the company re-measures the elevations at its piers and compares them with the readings filed at completion. Where the movement is at its own piers and inside the warranty terms, the fix is an adjustment, typically a re-lift or added shimming, at no charge or at the rate the contract states. Where the movement turns out to be in an unrepaired area or from an excluded cause, expect a new quote for that scope. Either way, the paperwork from the original job decides how the conversation goes, which is why it matters more than any promise made at the kitchen table.

Does a foundation warranty transfer to a new owner?

Yes, a transferable foundation warranty passes to the new owner, but the transfer usually has to be registered. Most companies require written notice within a set period after closing, often 30 to 90 days, and some charge a transfer fee. Ask for the transfer terms before you list the house, and hand the buyer the certificate and the original contract at closing.

What voids a foundation repair warranty?

A foundation repair warranty is usually voided by work that changes what the company installed or by conditions listed in the contract. Common exclusions are unrepaired plumbing leaks under the slab, major excavation or additions against the repaired wall, and another company altering or removing the piers. Cosmetic cracks elsewhere in the house do not void anything. Read the exclusions page before you sign, not after a claim.

Before you sign: the independent engineer option

Here is the part of the process most sales material skips. When a foundation repair company inspects your house for free, the person measuring your floors also sells the repair. That does not make the findings wrong, but it does mean the inspection is the first step of a sales process. Homeowners describe the same experience after difficult jobs: the visit felt like an evaluation, right up until the pier count started growing. An independent structural engineer is the alternative. Engineers sell reports, not piers, so their recommendation might be twenty piers, two piers, or monitoring only.

An independent evaluation typically costs around $350 to $600 and produces a stamped report with an elevation survey and a repair specification. You can hand that specification to any contractor and compare quotes on identical scope, which is the single strongest protection against overbuying. Consider the engineer first when a quote is large, when two companies propose very different pier counts, when you are buying or selling, or when the symptoms look minor but the proposed repair does not.

Should I get a structural engineer before a contractor?

Yes, if the stakes are high, an independent structural engineer is worth hiring before any contractor. For roughly $350 to $600 you get a stamped evaluation from someone who sells no repairs, so the pier count has no sales incentive behind it. Bring that report to contractors and every quote prices the same scope. For minor, localised problems, a contractor inspection alone is usually enough.

Paying for it: comparing financing offers without a sales pitch

Most companies offer payment plans or contractor-arranged financing, and none of it is free money. Compare any offer on three numbers: the interest rate or APR, the total you will repay over the full term, and whether there is a penalty for repaying early. A low monthly payment stretched over a long term can cost more than the repair protects in home value. One more number hides in many financing offers: the contractor discount fee. Some lending arrangements charge the contractor a percentage for placing the loan, and that cost can quietly be built into the quote you are financing. If a cash price and a financed price differ for the same scope, ask why, in writing. The answer tells you what the loan really costs before the interest rate even applies.

Is it better to finance foundation repair or pay cash?

Paying cash is cheapest whenever you can do it without draining your emergency fund. Financing makes sense when the repair stops active damage that would grow costlier, or when a sale or refinance depends on it. Compare the total repayment, not the monthly payment, and treat deferred-interest offers with care: miss the deadline and interest is often charged back to day one.

Frequently asked questions

How long does foundation repair take, from first call to finished job?

Most foundation repairs take two to four weeks end to end, with only one to three of those days spent working at your house. The rest is scheduling, permits where required, and material delivery. The inspection and quote normally happen within the first week. Large whole-house jobs or difficult access can stretch the repair itself to four or five days.

Is a foundation inspection really free?

A contractor's foundation inspection is usually free because it is part of that company's sales process. The inspector's job includes recommending a repair their company can sell. That does not make the findings false, but it is why a second opinion matters on large scopes. An independent structural engineer charges around $350 to $600 precisely because the report is the whole product.

Do I need a permit for foundation repair?

Permit rules vary by city, and many jurisdictions do require a permit for structural piering. Where one is required, the repair company normally applies for it as part of the job, and the fee should appear in your quote. Ask who is pulling the permit before work starts. Unpermitted structural work can surface later as a problem during a sale or an insurance claim.

What payment schedule is normal for foundation repair?

A normal payment schedule ties money to stages: a modest deposit on signing, a progress payment when piers are installed, and the balance after the lift, cleanup and walkthrough. Avoid any arrangement asking for the full amount before work begins. The schedule belongs in the written contract, along with what happens if either side cancels. Keep receipts with your warranty paperwork.

What if the repair work is not finished as promised?

Put the unfinished items in writing and tie them to the contract before the final payment moves. A short punch list with photos, dates and the specific contract clause for each item carries more weight than phone calls. If the company stalls, your warranty document and contract are the basis for complaints to the state licensing board or consumer protection office.

Will foundation repair increase my home's value?

Foundation repair protects value more than it adds value. A documented repair with a transferable warranty removes the discount buyers apply to a house with known foundation problems, and it clears a common lender and inspection hurdle at sale. Keep the elevation reports, contract and warranty certificate: that file is what an appraiser, a buyer's agent or an engineer will ask to see.
