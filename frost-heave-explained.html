Frost Heave Explained: Why Frozen Soil Lifts Foundations, Porches, and Slabs

Frost heave is the upward movement of soil, and anything resting on it, as the ground freezes. It happens when water in the soil migrates toward the freezing zone and forms layers of ice called ice lenses, which grow thicker through the winter and physically jack the soil upward. The lift can be strong enough to raise porches, garage slabs, fence posts, and shallow footings, sometimes by inches, and it relaxes as the ground thaws in spring.

Three ingredients must coincide: freezing temperatures reaching into the ground, frost-susceptible soil such as silts and fine clays that draw water upward, and a supply of water from a high water table, poor drainage, or melting snow. Remove any one ingredient and heave mostly stops. That is why deep footings on a well-drained site sit quietly through the same winter that lifts a shallow porch slab a few feet away.

This explainer covers how to tell frost heave from settlement, because they are often confused and the fixes differ, which parts of a property heave most, and what prevention actually works. What Is Frost Heave, Step by Step

Frost heave starts when a freezing front moves down into wet ground. Just ahead of that front, thin films of unfrozen water cling to soil particles, and the frozen soil above pulls that water upward through the fine pores of silt and clay. The arriving water freezes onto the bottom of a growing ice layer, an ice lens, which thickens as long as water keeps feeding it. Each lens lifts everything above it. Several lenses at different depths can stack their lift, which is how inches of movement accumulate over a single cold season.

The process explains its own preferences. Coarse gravel and clean sand barely heave because their large pores do not draw water upward. Silts heave the most, and fine clays heave moderately while also swelling. In Des Moines, freeze-thaw cycling works on older masonry and stone foundations every cold season, and in Cedar Rapids long, cold, snowy winters give lenses months of steady growth. Water supply decides severity: a wet fall followed by hard cold is the classic recipe for a bad heave year.

Can frost heave lift a foundation?

Yes, frost heave can lift a foundation, but it lifts shallow ones. Footings poured above the depth frost reaches, common on additions, garages, porches, and older construction, sit within the freezing zone and can be jacked upward by growing ice lenses. The main footings of a house built to local code sit below frost reach, so whole-house lifting is rare; edges, additions, and attached slabs are the usual victims.

How deep does frost go in my state?

No one can honestly give you a single number without your location. Frost penetration varies by state, county, and winter, from shallow in mild southern climates to very deep in the northern plains, and local building codes set footing depth from that data. Your building department publishes the required depth for your area. Footings must reach below it; that rule is the whole design defense against heave.

Frost Heave vs Settlement: Telling Them Apart

Frost heave vs settlement is the key diagnosis on this page, and the calendar tells most of it. Heave pushes structures up in winter and lets them settle back as thaw completes in spring, so the same door sticks in January and swings free in May. Settlement moves one way, down, in every season, and doors that stick from settlement stick in August too. Heave also tends to be patchy, lifting the exposed north side or the shallow end of a slab, while settlement follows the load and the weak soil.

The winter house lifting pattern adds a second test: compare levels across seasons, not within one. Photograph crack widths and door gaps in mid-winter and again after full thaw. If the house returns to its previous position, you are watching heave and the answer is prevention. If each spring ends slightly lower than the last, settlement is accumulating underneath the heave, and that combination needs an engineer, because piers installed against a misread diagnosis fix the wrong problem.

Does frost heave go away in spring?

Mostly, yes. Classic frost heave goes away as the ground thaws and ice lenses melt, and the lifted slab, porch, or footing settles back toward its original position by late spring. Two cautions: the return is often uneven, leaving a slight permanent tilt, and repeated seasons can ratchet a structure out of place a little at a time. Confirm the return happened; do not just assume it did.

How do I tell frost heave from settlement?

Watch the direction and the season. Heave moves things up, mostly in winter, and reverses at thaw; settlement moves things down and never reverses. Heave favors shallow, exposed elements like porch slabs and garage floors, and cracks from heave often partly close in spring. Settlement favors loaded footings, progresses year-round, and pairs with sloping floors and doors that stick in summer. Seasonal reversal is the strongest single tell.

What Heaves Most: Slabs, Porches, Additions, and Shallow Footings

Exposure decides the victim list. A frost heave garage floor is common because the slab is shallow, unheated at its edges, and fed by snowmelt and driveway runoff. A frost heave porch slab is almost expected in cold climates: small, shallow, unheated, and open to cold air on several sides. Additions and bump-outs heave where their footings were poured shallower than the main house's, a frequent shortcut in older work. Fence posts and deck footings are the neighborhood demonstration, tilting a little further each winter.

The main house foundation usually survives because its footings extend below frost penetration and the building's heat leaks into surrounding soil. Where the house does suffer is at junctions: the porch heaving against the wall, the garage slab lifting against the footing, the addition moving on a different schedule from the original structure. Those junctions crack, separate, and admit water, which then feeds next winter's lenses. In Madison, where historic near-east and near-west homes sit between lakes with a high water table nearby, water supply for heaving is rarely in short supply.

How do you protect shallow footings from frost?

Protect shallow footings by attacking the ingredients: keep soil beside them as dry as possible with grading, gutters, and downspout extensions; avoid watering or irrigation against the wall in late fall; and insulate where a professional recommends it, since insulation slows the freezing front's reach. For new work, the real protection is depth: footings extended below local frost penetration, on drained, compacted soil, as code requires.

My porch footing cracked from heaving. Should I worry?

Usually not a structural emergency, but not something to ignore. Patching the crack treats the symptom; the cause is a shallow, wet footing still inside the frost zone, and it will move again next winter. Stabilize the situation with drainage first. If the footing has cracked through, if the porch pulls on the house framing, or if each spring ends lower than the last, get it evaluated before rebuilding anything.

Prevention and Repair: What Actually Works

Prevention means removing one of the three ingredients, and water is the practical one. Grade soil so meltwater and roof runoff move away from shallow structures. Extend downspouts well past porches and slabs. Fix irrigation and plumbing leaks near footings, and avoid late-fall soaking of beds against the house. In the coldest exposures, rigid insulation placed by a professional, either vertically at a shallow footing or as a horizontal wing in the soil beside it, keeps ground heat in and frost out, and is a recognized frost-protection method for shallow foundations.

Repair follows the diagnosis. Heaved slabs and porches that return to position each spring usually need drainage correction and joint sealing, not replacement. Structures that have ratcheted out of place may need re-leveling or, for slabs, lifting such as slabjacking at $500 to $1,500 per section. Where a true footing has failed or settlement is mixed into the picture, an independent engineer evaluation at about $350 to $600 sorts heave from settlement before anyone sells you piers at $1,000 to $2,500 each. How is frost heave repaired?

Frost heave repair starts with drainage, not concrete: redirect roof and surface water away from the structure, let a full thaw complete, then re-measure. Porches and slabs that return to level usually need only joint sealing and the drainage fix. Slabs that stay tilted can be lifted; persistent footing problems on additions may need deeper support. Repairing before the thaw finishes means measuring a moving target.

Is it a mistake to repair heave damage in mid-winter?

Waiting is not neglect here; it is the correct move. A slab or porch measured mid-heave is at its worst and will improve on its own at thaw, so sealing, lifting, or replacing it in January fixes a position it will not keep. Use the winter to document, photograph, and plan drainage work. Schedule the professional evaluation for after full thaw, when measurements describe the structure's real resting position.

Frequently asked questions

Can frost heave cause permanent damage, or does it always settle back?

Yes, when it comes back unevenly. Soil rarely thaws and settles at exactly the same rate across a whole footing or slab, so a porch, slab section, or addition can finish spring slightly lower, or tilted, from where it started fall. Repeated seasons compound the difference. A one-time heave that fully returns is a warning; a structure that ends each spring lower than the last is accumulating real settlement.

What are the clearest frost heave signs?

Frost heave signs include a porch or step that rises and separates from the house in winter, garage doors that stop sealing at the bottom, new cracks appearing in cold months, fence posts tilting, and floors or slabs that feel higher on the outside edge. The calendar is the biggest clue: heave builds through the coldest weeks and begins relaxing as the ground thaws from the top down.

Does frost heave affect garage floors?

Yes, and it is a familiar complaint in Iowa and Wisconsin: a frost heave garage floor can lift enough to crack or change how the door closes, then drop back in spring. The slab edge near the overhead door is most exposed to cold air and frost-susceptible fill. If the floor returns to level and cracks stay hairline, monitor it; a floor that settles lower each year needs evaluation.

What should I do about a porch slab that heaves every winter?

A frost heave porch slab is common because porches are shallow, unheated, and exposed on multiple sides, close to ideal heaving conditions. Keep water away from its base, seal the joint where it meets the house so meltwater cannot run underneath, and improve drainage on its downhill side. If the porch is dragging on the house's framing or its footing has cracked through, have it evaluated rather than repeatedly patching.

How do I prevent frost heave around my house?

The single most effective step is drainage: keep water out of the soil beside and under shallow structures. Clean gutters, extend downspouts away from the foundation, regrade soil that funnels water toward the house, and fix any plumbing or irrigation leaks near footings. Heave needs water to build ice lenses, so a drier soil profile in fall means a quieter winter, whatever the temperature does.

When does frost heave need a professional?

Call a professional when the main house is lifting rather than a porch or slab, when cracks are wide, horizontal, offset, or still growing in spring, or when doors and floors do not return to normal after thaw. Start with an independent structural engineer, typically $350 to $600, if the pattern is unclear. Pier work, if a footing truly needs it, runs $1,000 to $2,500 per pier installed.
