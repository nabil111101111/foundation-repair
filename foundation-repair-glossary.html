Foundation repair glossary: the terms on your quote, in plain English

Foundation repair has more jargon than it needs, and the jargon does real harm. Homeowners sign quotes full of words they half-recognise, compare two companies that use different terms for the same work, and nod along in inspections rather than ask what a word means. This glossary exists to end that. Every term below is one you are likely to meet on an inspection report, a quote or a warranty document.

Definitions here are written the way we would want them explained to us: one or two sentences, no sales angle, and a note where the term is used loosely in the trade. Where two terms compete, like slabjacking and mudjacking, the entries say which one contains the other. Keep this page open next to any quote you are reading; the words on the paper should all be findable here.

Piers and underpinning terms

Most structural repairs come down to getting the house's weight onto soil that can hold it, and these are the words for the hardware that does it. If a quote names a pier system, this cluster tells you what you are actually buying.

  • Underpinning — Adding new support under an existing foundation so the house's load reaches soil strong enough to carry it. Most structural foundation repair is underpinning of one kind or another, usually done with piers.
  • Helical pier — A steel shaft with spiral plates that is screwed into the ground until torque readings show it has reached load-bearing soil. Brackets then connect it to the footing, so the pier can lift or stabilise the home.
  • Push pier — A steel tube driven into the ground in sections using the weight of the house itself as resistance, until it reaches stable soil or rock. Also called a resistance pier or driven pier.
  • Concrete pier — A pier made of stacked concrete cylinders pressed into the ground, or of concrete poured into a dug shaft. Common in some regions and cheaper per pier, with performance that depends heavily on depth and installation quality.
  • Bearing strata — The layer of dense soil or rock, deep enough to carry the load a pier transfers down to it. Reaching bearing strata, rather than hitting a fixed depth, is what makes a pier installation finished.
  • Deadload — The permanent weight of the structure itself: its walls, roof, floors and foundation. Push piers rely on deadload to drive against, which is why very light structures can be poor candidates for them.
  • Torque verification — Reading the installation torque of a helical pier as it is screwed in, to confirm the soil is resisting enough to support the rated load. It is the helical installer's equivalent of a depth record.

Lifting and levelling terms

These terms cover the work of getting floors and slabs back up, or at least stopping them going further down. The naming is genuinely confusing in this corner of the trade, so the entries flag which words are synonyms and which are parents of the others.

  • Slabjacking — Lifting sunken concrete by pumping material under the slab through small drilled holes until the surface is level again. Used on ground-floor slabs, driveways and porches; it is the umbrella term for mudjacking and polyjacking.
  • Mudjacking — Slabjacking with a pumped slurry of soil, cement and water. The material is inexpensive and the holes are larger; the added weight can matter on weak soils, and typical lifespans run shorter than foam.
  • Polyjacking — Slabjacking with expanding polyurethane foam injected through small holes. The foam is light, cures in minutes and resists water, at a higher material cost than slurry. Also called foam lifting or poly lifting.
  • House levelling — Returning a home's floors toward their original plane by lifting low areas on piers or supports and re-shimming where needed. Level within tolerance is the goal; perfectly flat is not a realistic promise on an older home.
  • Re-shimming — Adding or adjusting the shims between a pier-and-beam home's piers and its beams, to take up settlement and level the floor. Cheap when the piers are sound; pointless when the piers themselves are sinking.
  • Footing — The widened concrete base at the bottom of a foundation wall, or under a pier, spreading the load over more soil. Most underpinning brackets attach at the footing, which is why quotes mention it so often.
  • Grade beam — A reinforced concrete beam, usually at ground level, that spans between piers and carries the wall above it. Common under slab edges in expansive-soil regions, where it bridges soil that moves with the seasons.

Soil and movement terms

Foundations rarely fail on their own; the ground moves first. These eight terms describe the ways soil and water do the moving, and at least one of them is behind almost every problem described anywhere on this site.

  • Differential settlement — One part of a foundation sinking more than another. It is the difference, not the sinking itself, that cracks walls, racks door frames and slopes floors, and measuring it is what most foundation diagnosis is for.
  • Expansive soil — Clay-rich soil that swells when wet and shrinks when dry. That seasonal change in volume lifts and drops foundations a little at a time, and it drives much of the foundation damage in Texas and Louisiana.
  • Shrink-swell — The repeated cycle of expansive soil swelling in wet periods and shrinking in dry ones. An open gap between the soil and the foundation during a dry summer is the classic visible sign of shrink-swell at work.
  • Frost heave — Soil lifting as the water in it freezes and expands into lenses of ice. Heave can raise footings and slabs in winter, then drop them unevenly at thaw, which is why northern foundations are built below the frost line.
  • Karst — Terrain formed where water dissolves limestone or similar rock, leaving voids, channels and sinkholes underground. In karst country a foundation can lose support suddenly when a void collapses, the pattern behind limestone settlement around Lexington.
  • Subsidence — Ground sinking across an area because material below it is removed or compacted, through groundwater withdrawal, decaying organic soil or dissolving rock. Unlike one house settling, subsidence affects whole neighbourhoods, as in parts of New Orleans.
  • Hydrostatic pressure — The push of groundwater against a basement wall or under a slab, growing stronger as the water table rises. It bows walls and forces water through cracks and joints, and it is managed with drainage rather than sealant alone.
  • Cut and fill — A building lot graded by cutting soil from the high side and filling the low side. The filled side is looser than native soil and often settles faster, a common cause of differential settlement in newer subdivisions.

Walls, water and wood terms

Below the ground floor, three different fights happen at once: walls against soil pressure, drainage against water, and wood against moisture. This cluster names the parts on all three fronts, including the pieces an inspector will point at in your crawl space.

  • Carbon-fiber strap — A strip of carbon-fiber fabric bonded to a basement wall with epoxy, to resist further inward bowing. Straps stabilise walls with modest movement; they do not pull a wall back straight, and they cannot rescue a wall that has moved too far.
  • Wall anchor — A steel rod run from a basement wall out to an anchor plate buried in the yard, tightened to resist bowing and sometimes to draw the wall back over time. Anchors need yard access and stable soil beyond the backfilled zone.
  • I-beam brace — A vertical steel beam fixed against a bowing basement wall, anchored to the floor slab and the joists above, that stops further inward movement. Braces cost a little floor space and stabilise a wall rather than straightening it.
  • French drain — A gravel-filled trench holding a perforated pipe that collects groundwater and carries it away from the foundation. Interior versions run along the basement footing to a sump; exterior versions intercept the water before it reaches the wall.
  • Sump — A pit at the low point of a drainage system, where collected water gathers so a sump pump can lift it out and discharge it away from the house. A sump manages water; it does not stop soil from moving.
  • Encapsulation — Sealing a crawl space with a heavy vapour barrier over the ground and walls, closing the vents, and usually dehumidifying or conditioning the air inside. Encapsulation controls moisture and protects wood; it does not repair structure that has already failed.
  • Sill plate — The wooden member laid flat along the top of the foundation wall, anchoring the framing above to the foundation below. Sills sit exactly where moisture collects, which is why they are a common rot point in older crawl-space homes.
  • Girder — A main horizontal beam, usually running under the centre of a house, that carries the floor joists and is itself carried by piers. A sagging girder produces the classic low spot in the middle of a room.
  • Joist — One of the parallel horizontal timbers that span between beams or walls and carry the floor directly. Joists fail by rotting at their ends, by being cut through for plumbing, or by spanning farther than they should.
  • Stem wall — The short wall of concrete or block that rises from the footing around a home's perimeter, forming the crawl space and carrying the house's edges. Stem wall cracks are read like any foundation cracks: pattern first, then width.

Quotes, diagnosis and warranty terms

The last cluster is paperwork, and it matters as much as the hardware. These three terms decide whether the document you sign can be checked, compared and enforced later, which is where most foundation disputes are actually won or lost.

  • Elevation survey — Measuring floor heights across a house with a precision instrument, to map exactly where it has sunk and by how much. It turns 'the floor feels sloped' into numbers that inspectors, engineers, quotes and warranties can all refer back to.
  • Itemised quote — A written price broken into named lines: pier count and unit price, excavation, permits, drainage, restoration and warranty terms. Itemisation is what makes two quotes comparable; without it, a quote is a sales document rather than an estimate.
  • Transferable warranty — A repair warranty that can pass to the next owner when the home is sold, usually after registering the transfer with the company within a set period. It is worth pinning down before work starts, not at closing.

Frequently asked questions

What is underpinning, in plain English?

Underpinning means adding support under an existing foundation so the load of the house reaches stronger soil. In practice it usually means installing piers beside or under the footing and connecting them with brackets. It is the umbrella term for most structural foundation repair, and when a quote says underpinning, it should still name the pier system being used.

What is the difference between a helical pier and a push pier?

A helical pier is screwed into the ground with a hydraulic motor, and its holding power is read from installation torque. A push pier is driven into the ground using the weight of the house itself as resistance. Both end at stable soil or rock. Helicals suit lighter structures and tight access; push piers suit heavier homes with enough weight to drive against.

What does 'transferable warranty' mean when I sell my house?

It means the warranty on foundation work can pass to the buyer when the house changes hands, instead of expiring at the sale. The transfer usually has to be registered with the repair company, sometimes within a set period after closing. A transferable warranty with its paperwork in order is a selling point, because the buyer inherits protection rather than a story.

Is slabjacking the same thing as mudjacking?

Slabjacking is the umbrella term for lifting sunken concrete by pumping material underneath it. Pump a cement slurry and most people call it mudjacking; pump expanding polyurethane foam and it is polyjacking or foam lifting. The goal is identical and the differences are material, hole size, cure time and price, which is why quotes should name the material rather than just the method family.

What is differential settlement, and why does it matter more than settlement?

Settlement is a house sinking; differential settlement is one part sinking more than another, and that difference is what does the damage. A home that settles evenly may show almost no symptoms, while an inch of difference across a wall cracks masonry, racks doors and slopes floors. Every diagnosis in foundation repair is really about finding where the differential is and why.

Do I need to know these terms to get a fair quote?

No. You do not need the vocabulary to be treated fairly, because a good quote explains itself in plain language and itemises the work. The terms help at two moments: reading a quote, where pier type, bearing and warranty language decide what you are actually buying, and comparing quotes, where different words sometimes hide the same work or the same words hide different work.
